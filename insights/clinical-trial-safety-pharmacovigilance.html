Clinical Trial Safety

Clinical Trial Safety and Pharmacovigilance: From Adverse Event Collection to DSUR

A complete guide to clinical-trial AEs, SAEs, sponsor and investigator duties, SUSAR assessment, expedited reporting, RSI governance and DSUR.

By Dr. Harsha, Doctorate in Pharmacy 2026-07-235 min read

A participant is hospitalised during a blinded clinical trial. The investigator considers the event unrelated. The sponsor’s medical reviewer sees a plausible mechanism. The reference safety information lists a similar diagnosis—but with a different severity and outcome.

The case does not become a SUSAR because one person selects a checkbox. It becomes a regulatory decision only after seriousness, causality and expectedness are assessed against controlled evidence.

Clinical-trial safety is therefore not case forwarding. It is continuous protection of participants through traceable medical judgement.

The investigator observes the participant. The sponsor sees the development programme. Safe decisions require both perspectives—but accountability cannot become blurred.

Regulatory foundation: ICH E6(R3); ICH E2A; ICH E2F; EMA clinical-trial safety reporting

Why clinical-trial safety is different from post-marketing pharmacovigilance

During development, exposure is protocol-controlled, data are actively collected and the safety profile is still evolving. The protocol, investigator’s brochure, reference safety information, randomisation and study endpoints shape the assessment.

Post-marketing terminology should not be copied mechanically into trials. Clinical-trial safety follows pre-authorisation requirements, study documents and regional rules.

The safety-information chain

The chain begins with the participant and site, continues through investigator assessment and sponsor receipt, and extends to medical review, case processing, aggregate evaluation, regulatory reporting, investigator communication and safety governance.

A delay at any interface can consume the short reporting period available after sponsor awareness.

  • Participant observation and clinical care.
  • Site documentation and investigator assessment.
  • Rapid SAE transmission to the sponsor.
  • Sponsor-wide medical and aggregate evaluation.
  • SUSAR reporting and investigator communication.
  • Ongoing benefit–risk and trial action.

AE, AR, SAE and SAR are not interchangeable

An adverse event does not require a causal relationship. An adverse reaction carries a reasonable possibility of causal association. Seriousness is based on outcome criteria and medical importance—not symptom intensity.

A serious adverse reaction becomes a SUSAR only when it is also unexpected against the applicable reference safety information.

Investigator responsibilities

The investigator protects the participant, provides appropriate medical care, documents events and reports serious adverse events promptly to the sponsor according to the protocol and applicable rules. Immediate reporting should not wait for complete records; follow-up can provide missing detail.

The investigator’s causality assessment is essential source evidence. It does not eliminate the sponsor’s obligation to conduct its own assessment.

Sponsor responsibilities

The sponsor must maintain systems to receive safety information, evaluate individual and aggregate data, determine reportability, submit required reports, inform investigators and authorities, update safety documents and act when risk changes.

Delegation to a CRO or vendor does not remove sponsor responsibility. Agreements, oversight, reconciliation and escalation must protect the reporting clock.

Seriousness, causality and expectedness

These are three separate decisions. Seriousness evaluates outcome. Causality evaluates whether there is a reasonable possibility that the investigational product caused the event. Expectedness compares the nature and severity of the reaction with the approved reference safety information.

A known clinical concept is not automatically expected if the observed specificity or severity is inconsistent with the RSI.

The SUSAR decision

A SUSAR is suspected, unexpected and serious. Each component must be supported. The sponsor should retain investigator and sponsor causality positions, the effective RSI version, medical reasoning and any unblinding decision.

Fatal or life-threatening SUSARs generally follow the shorter seven-day initial reporting window under major frameworks, while other SUSARs generally follow a fifteen-day window. Exact destinations and follow-up obligations remain jurisdiction-specific.

Blinding and controlled unblinding

Blinding protects trial integrity, but participant safety and regulatory compliance can require treatment identification. Unblinding should follow the protocol and controlled procedures, be limited to those who need the information and preserve evidence of who knew what and when.

Operational convenience is not a reason to break the blind; avoidance of a reportable safety assessment is not a reason to preserve it.

Reference safety information governance

The RSI must be identifiable, approved and version-controlled. Expectedness assessment should use the version applicable under the trial and jurisdiction, with clear effective dates and transition rules.

Safety reviewers need access to the exact wording—not an informal list of expected events.

Follow-up and case quality

High-value follow-up addresses diagnosis, onset, seriousness criterion, dose, treatment dates, dechallenge, rechallenge, alternative causes, relevant tests, concomitant therapies and outcome. Requests should reflect the clinical question.

Source-data corrections, case amendments and significant follow-up must remain attributable and auditable.

Aggregate assessment and DSUR

Some risks become visible only across participants, treatment arms, studies or external sources. The DSUR integrates interval safety data with cumulative exposure, important findings, actions and evolving benefit–risk.

A programme can be compliant with individual SUSAR timelines and still fail participant protection if it does not evaluate the pattern.

Actions beyond reporting

New information may require urgent safety measures, protocol amendment, informed-consent revision, investigator’s brochure update, additional monitoring, enrolment pause or trial termination. Reporting is communication; risk control is the objective.

Governance should document the evidence, accountable decision, implementation and confirmation that sites received and acted on critical information.

Technology and AI with accountable review

Automation can detect SAE language, reconcile EDC and safety databases, prioritise follow-up and support expectedness comparison. It must not invent causality, overlook negation or replace medically accountable SUSAR determination.

Validation should challenge false negatives, endpoint events, blinded data, changing RSI versions, multilingual reports and cross-study duplicates.

The inspection test

An inspector may trace one event from source record to site report, sponsor awareness, medical assessment, RSI comparison, submission, acknowledgement and investigator communication. Every timestamp and judgement must reconcile.

A complete case is not enough if the organisation cannot reconstruct why the participant-safety decision was made.

Inspection-ready checklist

  • Protocol defines safety collection and reporting.
  • Investigators understand immediate SAE reporting.
  • Sponsor awareness is consistently dated.
  • Seriousness and severity remain distinct.
  • Investigator and sponsor causality are retained.
  • RSI versions and effective dates are controlled.
  • SUSAR decisions are medically documented.
  • Unblinding follows a controlled process.
  • Submissions and acknowledgements reconcile.
  • Individual cases feed aggregate review.
  • DSUR data are cross-functional and traceable.
  • Risk actions reach sites and participants.

Frequently asked questions

What is clinical-trial pharmacovigilance?

The collection, assessment, reporting and cumulative oversight of safety information arising during clinical development to protect participants and inform benefit–risk decisions.

Is every SAE a SUSAR?

No. A SUSAR must be serious, suspected to be causally related and unexpected against the applicable RSI.

Can a sponsor disagree with investigator causality?

Yes, but both assessments and the sponsor’s medical reasoning should be retained, and regional reporting rules must be applied.

What is the difference between serious and severe?

Seriousness is based on regulatory outcome criteria; severity describes intensity.

Does expedited reporting replace aggregate review?

No. Individual reporting and programme-level cumulative evaluation are complementary controls.

Conclusion

Clinical-trial safety succeeds when the information from one participant can change the protection of every participant who follows.

That requires more than rapid data entry. It requires clear roles, controlled reference information, medical reasoning, global awareness and action proportional to risk.

The reporting clock protects compliance. The safety system must protect the participant.
