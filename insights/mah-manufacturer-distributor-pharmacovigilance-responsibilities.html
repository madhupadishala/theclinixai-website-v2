PV Agreements & Partner Governance

MAH, Manufacturer, Distributor and Licensing-Partner Pharmacovigilance Responsibilities

Understand MAH, manufacturer, distributor, affiliate, licensor, licensee and vendor responsibilities for pharmacovigilance.

TheClinixAI PV Science & Operations 2026-07-234 min read

A medicine may be manufactured by one company, licensed by another, distributed by a third and marketed by several local entities. The patient does not know this structure—and should not bear its risk.

Any relevant organisation can become the first point of contact for safety information. The network must ensure that the accountable pharmacovigilance system receives it.

Corporate structure can divide commercial ownership. It cannot divide the patient’s safety event into someone else’s problem.

Regulatory foundation: GVP Module I; GVP Module II; GVP Module VI; EMA PV system Q&A

The MAH

The MAH is accountable for operating the PV system for its authorised products and meeting applicable obligations. It may delegate activities, but must retain oversight, access, governance and evidence.

The MAH should maintain visibility across vendors, affiliates, distributors, manufacturers and licensing partners.

Manufacturers and contract manufacturers

Manufacturing personnel may receive complaints involving adverse events, exposure, medication error or product-quality concerns. Procedures and training should enable recognition and prompt forwarding.

Where quality defects and adverse events overlap, quality and PV investigations must remain connected.

Distributors, wholesalers and commercial partners

Distributors may receive reports from pharmacies, healthcare professionals or patients. Commercial agreements should define recognition, minimum collection, transfer, contact points and timelines.

A distributor should not assess a report out of scope and decide independently that it is non-reportable unless responsibility and competence are formally defined.

Licensors and licensees

Licensing arrangements may split territories, submission obligations, database ownership, signals, aggregate reports and regulatory communication. Global safety strategy and local obligations must remain aligned.

Both directions of exchange require control because each party may receive unique information.

Affiliates and local safety units

Affiliates understand local language, sources, authorities and market context. They require clear responsibilities for intake, literature, submission, follow-up, escalation and local regulatory intelligence.

Headquarters oversight must not erase local legal obligations.

Service providers and CROs

Vendors may process cases, monitor literature, manage systems or author reports. Contracts, procedures, access, training, metrics, audits and CAPA should reflect the risk of the activity.

The MAH should independently verify performance rather than rely only on vendor certification.

All employees as potential recipients

Safety information can reach sales, medical affairs, quality, legal, digital, market research, patient-support and executive teams. Enterprise training should be role-appropriate and emphasise recognition and immediate routing.

The first recipient should collect minimum information safely, not perform unauthorised medical assessment.

Responsibility mapping

A RACI alone is insufficient if it lacks clocks, systems, evidence and escalation. Each responsibility should identify accountable entity, performing role, source, output, timeline, quality control and backup.

Inspection and audit accountability

Parties should retain records and support audits and inspections according to scope. The MAH must be able to explain the entire outsourced system and demonstrate oversight.

Saying 'the vendor performs that activity' is not an inspection response unless the MAH can show how it knows the activity is controlled.

Inspection-ready checklist

  • MAH accountability is explicit.
  • Manufacturers route AE-containing complaints.
  • Distributors have trained intake pathways.
  • Licensor/licensee responsibilities are bidirectional.
  • Affiliates retain local obligations.
  • Vendors are governed by evidence.
  • Enterprise functions recognise safety information.
  • RACI includes timelines and systems.
  • Audit and inspection support are defined.

Frequently asked questions

Who is ultimately responsible for an authorised product’s PV system?

The MAH holds accountability under the applicable framework, even when activities are delegated.

Must manufacturers report adverse events they receive?

Relevant safety information should be identified and forwarded according to applicable obligations and agreements; manufacturing and quality functions require controlled PV interfaces.

Can distributors decide a case is invalid?

Only if responsibility, criteria, training and governance are formally defined; otherwise information should be promptly transferred for PV assessment.

Who submits cases in a licensing arrangement?

The agreement must assign responsibilities by product, territory and report type while preserving all applicable obligations.

Do all employees need PV training?

Personnel who may receive safety information need role-appropriate awareness and routing training.

Conclusion

The pharmacovigilance network is only as strong as its least controlled interface. Responsibility must be written, understood, measured and tested from the first recipient to the final regulatory action.

The right question is not, “Who received the report?” It is, “How did the accountable PV system know—and can we prove when?”
