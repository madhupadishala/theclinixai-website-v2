PV Agreements & Partner Governance

MAH, Manufacturer, Distributor and Licensing-Partner Pharmacovigilance Responsibilities

Understand MAH, manufacturer, distributor, affiliate, licensor, licensee and vendor responsibilities for pharmacovigilance.

By Dr. Surya, Doctorate in Pharmacy 2026-07-234 min read

A single medicine can be manufactured by one company, licensed by another, distributed through a third, and marketed by several local entities layered on top of all that. The patient at the end of this chain has no idea any of that structure exists — and they shouldn't have to bear the risk of it either.

Any one of those organisations can end up being the first point of contact when a safety event happens. The whole network's job is to make sure that information actually reaches the pharmacovigilance system that's accountable for it.

Corporate structure can divide up commercial ownership however it likes. It cannot divide a patient's safety event into somebody else's problem.

Regulatory foundation: GVP Module I; GVP Module II; GVP Module VI; EMA PV system Q&A

The MAH

The MAH carries accountability for operating the PV system behind its authorised products and meeting every applicable obligation. It's free to delegate activities to others — plenty do — but it has to hold on to oversight, access, governance, and the evidence that proves all three.

That means real visibility across vendors, affiliates, distributors, manufacturers and licensing partners — not a assumption that everyone downstream is handling things correctly.

Manufacturers and contract manufacturers

People on the manufacturing floor sometimes end up hearing about adverse events, exposure, medication error, or product-quality concerns before anyone in PV does. Procedures and training need to make sure they recognise what they're hearing and forward it promptly, rather than treating it purely as a quality issue.

Where a quality defect and an adverse event turn out to be connected, the quality investigation and the PV investigation need to stay linked — I've seen them run in parallel with no communication, and that's a real gap.

Distributors, wholesalers and commercial partners

Distributors are often the ones who hear directly from pharmacies, healthcare professionals or patients. The commercial agreement needs to spell out how they recognise a report, what minimum information they collect, how it gets transferred, who the contact point is, and what the timeline looks like.

Unless responsibility and competence have been formally defined, a distributor shouldn't be making its own call that a report falls outside scope and doesn't need to go anywhere.

Licensors and licensees

Licensing arrangements can split up territories, submission obligations, database ownership, signal responsibilities, aggregate reports, and communication with regulators. Whatever the split, the global safety strategy and the local obligations still need to line up with each other.

Information needs to flow in both directions under control, because each party can end up holding something unique the other one doesn't have.

Affiliates and local safety units

Affiliates bring something headquarters usually doesn't have — local language, local sources, local regulators, local market context. They need clearly assigned responsibility for intake, literature, submission, follow-up, escalation and local regulatory intelligence.

Central oversight from headquarters is important, but it can't be allowed to quietly override a local legal obligation.

Service providers and CROs

Vendors might process cases, monitor literature, run systems, or author reports on your behalf. Contracts, procedures, access controls, training, metrics, audits and CAPA all need to reflect how much risk sits in that particular activity.

The MAH should be independently verifying vendor performance — not simply relying on a certificate the vendor hands over once a year.

All employees as potential recipients

Safety information doesn't only land on the PV team's desk. It can reach sales, medical affairs, quality, legal, digital, market research, patient-support, even executives. Training across the enterprise needs to be role-appropriate, with the emphasis on recognising what they're hearing and routing it fast.

Whoever receives it first should collect the minimum safe information — not attempt an unauthorised medical assessment of their own.

Responsibility mapping

A RACI chart on its own doesn't get you very far if it's missing clocks, systems, evidence, and an escalation path. Every responsibility on that map needs an accountable entity, a performing role, a source, an output, a timeline, a quality check, and a backup for when the primary owner isn't available.

Inspection and audit accountability

Every party in the chain needs to retain records and support audits and inspections within their scope. The MAH, specifically, has to be able to explain the entire outsourced system end to end and demonstrate genuine oversight of it.

"The vendor handles that" is not an answer an inspector will accept, unless the MAH can also show exactly how it knows that activity is actually under control.

Inspection-ready checklist

  • MAH accountability is explicit.
  • Manufacturers route AE-containing complaints.
  • Distributors have trained intake pathways.
  • Licensor/licensee responsibilities are bidirectional.
  • Affiliates retain local obligations.
  • Vendors are governed by evidence.
  • Enterprise functions recognise safety information.
  • RACI includes timelines and systems.
  • Audit and inspection support are defined.

Frequently asked questions

Who is ultimately responsible for an authorised product’s PV system?

The MAH holds accountability under the applicable framework, even when activities are delegated.

Must manufacturers report adverse events they receive?

Relevant safety information should be identified and forwarded according to applicable obligations and agreements; manufacturing and quality functions require controlled PV interfaces.

Can distributors decide a case is invalid?

Only if responsibility, criteria, training and governance are formally defined; otherwise information should be promptly transferred for PV assessment.

Who submits cases in a licensing arrangement?

The agreement must assign responsibilities by product, territory and report type while preserving all applicable obligations.

Do all employees need PV training?

Personnel who may receive safety information need role-appropriate awareness and routing training.

Conclusion

A pharmacovigilance network is only ever as strong as its weakest, least-controlled interface. Responsibility has to be written down, understood by the people holding it, measured, and actually tested — from whoever first receives a report all the way through to the final regulatory action.

The question that matters isn't "Who received the report?" It's "How did the accountable PV system find out — and can we prove exactly when?"
