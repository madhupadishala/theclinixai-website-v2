PV Agreements & Partner Governance

Pharmacovigilance Agreements and Partner Governance: A Complete Compliance Guide

A complete guide to PV agreements, SDEAs, partner responsibilities, case exchange, reconciliation, vendor oversight and MAH accountability.

By Dr. Surya, Doctorate in Pharmacy 2026-07-235 min read

Here's a scenario I've reviewed more than once in slightly different forms: a patient reports a serious adverse event to a distributor. The distributor logs it as a product complaint and forwards it to the manufacturer ten days later. The manufacturer assumes the licensing partner will pick it up from there. The partner never actually receives it.

Every organisation in that chain can point to a signed contract. Not one of them can prove the safety information reached the accountable pharmacovigilance system on time.

This isn't primarily a case-processing failure. It's a governance failure, and it happened exactly at the interface between two organisations — which is precisely where these things tend to go wrong.

Patient safety doesn't recognise corporate boundaries. The regulatory clock certainly won't pause while partners debate whose job it was.

Why partner governance is a core PV control

A single medicinal product can involve an MAH, a licence partner, a manufacturer, distributors, wholesalers, affiliates, CROs, call centres, digital vendors, and other service providers. Safety information can walk in through any one of those doors.

The PV system needs to define, clearly, how that information gets identified, transferred, processed, submitted, reconciled and escalated. Contract language supports this — but the actual goal is uninterrupted safety oversight, not a well-worded clause.

Delegation does not remove accountability

An MAH can subcontract pharmacovigilance activities out. It cannot subcontract away its responsibility for the PV system or the underlying obligations. I treat that as a fixed principle behind every outsourcing or licensing decision I review.

The responsible organisation needs to know exactly what was delegated, to whom, for which products and territories, against which standards, and how that performance actually gets verified independently — not just assumed.

From commercial contract to operational safety agreement

Commercial agreements are often very clear on ownership, supply, territories and payment — and considerably vaguer on PV responsibilities. A dedicated safety data exchange agreement, or an equivalent PV section, is what actually translates the relationship into operational control.

That agreement has to align with the real regulatory obligations, the product's lifecycle stage, the countries involved, the data sources, and the systems actually in use. Generic clauses copied wholesale from a previous partnership rarely hold up.

Essential responsibility domains

A responsibility matrix worth the name covers every safety-critical activity — not just spontaneous case reporting, which is where most people's attention naturally goes first.

  • Safety-information intake and employee awareness.
  • Case transfer, follow-up, duplicate management and medical review.
  • Literature monitoring and local-language sources.
  • Regulatory submission ownership and acknowledgements.
  • Clinical-trial and post-marketing boundaries.
  • Signal detection, validation, assessment and communication.
  • Aggregate reports, RMPs and safety variations.
  • Reconciliation, compliance metrics, deviations and CAPA.
  • Inspection, audit, business continuity and termination support.

Awareness date and exchange timelines

The agreement should say precisely when each party is considered "aware," what minimum information is enough to trigger a transfer, and whether the clock runs in calendar days or business days. Internal timelines need to leave real room for assessment and submission afterward.

A transfer deadline can sit technically inside the regulatory timeline and still be operationally unsafe, if it leaves the receiving party no actual time to search for duplicates, complete medical review, and transmit.

Product, territory and source scope

Scope should name products, active substances, formulations, indications, studies, countries and legal entities explicitly. It should also define which sources are covered — spontaneous, solicited, literature, digital, social media, medical information, complaints, market research — wherever each applies.

Ambiguity here gets dangerous fast during an acquisition, a divestment, co-marketing, or an indication expansion, because the operational scope can shift well before anyone gets around to updating the agreement.

Reconciliation as proof of completeness

Reconciliation is the control that actually tests whether the expected information crossed the interface. It should run in both directions, resolve discrepancies, assess how late anything was, and document how it closed.

"No cases were exchanged" is not reconciliation on its own — not unless each party has independently confirmed its own source population, period, and search method behind that statement.

Oversight, metrics and governance meetings

Governance review should cover exchange timeliness, rejected files, quality defects, missing follow-up, reconciliation discrepancies, overdue actions, deviations, and regulatory impact. Simply showing up to the meeting isn't evidence of oversight.

Set thresholds that actually trigger escalation and CAPA. A repeat late exchange is a system weakness, not just a monthly operational annoyance to note and move past.

Change control

Regulatory change, a new product, a new territory, a new vendor, a system migration, a merger, a divestment, even a simple contact change — any of these can quietly invalidate an existing agreement. Every relationship needs a defined change-notification mechanism and an accountable owner.

The operational process on the ground and the contract on file should never drift apart without anyone noticing.

Termination and data transition

Termination clauses need to address case cut-off, open follow-up, database transfer, source documents, submissions, signals, aggregate reports, RMP commitments, records, retention, privacy, and inspection support.

Exiting a commercial relationship does not erase the historical pharmacovigilance obligations that came with it.

A practical failure scenario

A service provider reports 100% on-time exchange, because it's measuring delivery from the moment a case lands in its own safety mailbox. During an audit, the MAH discovers that local medical-information teams sometimes sit on reports for internal clarification before forwarding them to that mailbox at all. The metric only starts counting once the provider has the case — the uncontrolled time before that point is completely invisible.

Fixing this needs enterprise training, proper source mapping, real day-zero governance, a clarified agreement, a retrospective impact assessment, and a metric that starts at first organisational awareness — not a reminder email to the vendor.

Technology and AI across partners

Structured gateways and APIs cut down manual delay, but the mappings, acknowledgements, error queues and version compatibility behind them still need validation and ongoing monitoring. AI can help classify incoming communications or flag missing data — but someone still has to define who's accountable for false negatives, how evidence is retained, and where human review sits in the loop.

No partner should assume an automated transfer succeeded just because it was sent. Wait for the receiving system to confirm acceptance.

Inspection-ready checklist

  • All products, territories and entities are scoped.
  • Responsibilities cover the full PV lifecycle.
  • Awareness and transfer clocks are unambiguous.
  • Submission and acknowledgement ownership are defined.
  • Reconciliation is bidirectional and evidenced.
  • Metrics begin at the correct awareness point.
  • Changes trigger controlled agreement review.
  • Deviations and CAPA cross organisational boundaries.
  • Termination protects ongoing and historical obligations.
  • MAH oversight remains documented.

Frequently asked questions

What is a pharmacovigilance agreement?

A controlled agreement defining how parties identify, exchange, process, submit, reconcile and govern safety information and related PV obligations.

Is an SDEA always legally required as a document with that name?

Requirements and contracting models vary, but delegated and shared PV responsibilities must be clearly defined and controlled. A dedicated SDEA is a common operational mechanism.

Can an MAH outsource responsibility?

It can outsource activities, but it retains accountability for its PV system and regulatory obligations.

What should be reconciled?

Cases, transfers, submissions and other governed safety deliverables according to the relationship’s scope and risk.

When should an agreement be revised?

When products, territories, entities, processes, systems, regulations or delegated responsibilities materially change.

Conclusion

The strongest pharmacovigilance agreement isn't the longest one on file. It's the one that actually stops a safety report, a signal, or a regulatory obligation from quietly disappearing in the space between two organisations.

A contract defines responsibility on paper. Governance is what proves that responsibility is actually being carried out in practice.

If every partner in the chain believes someone else owns the clock, the patient ends up being the only one still carrying the risk.
