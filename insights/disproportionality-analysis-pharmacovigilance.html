Disproportionality Analysis in Pharmacovigilance: PRR, ROR and IC Explained

Understand PRR, ROR, IC, spontaneous-reporting bias and the correct use of disproportionality analysis in pharmacovigilance.

By Wany Williams, BDS 2026-07-234 min read

A disproportionality score can be mathematically correct and clinically meaningless. It can also reveal a pattern no individual reviewer would notice.

The difference lies in how the result is interpreted.

Disproportionality measures reporting imbalance. It does not measure incidence, absolute risk or causality.

Regulatory foundation: GVP IX Addendum; GVP IX; EudraVigilance

What disproportionality analysis measures

Disproportionality compares the reporting of a drug–event pair with reporting of other drug–event combinations in the same database. It is designed for hypothesis generation within spontaneous-report data.

PRR, ROR and IC

The proportional reporting ratio compares event proportions for the product versus comparators. The reporting odds ratio uses odds from a two-by-two table. The information component is a Bayesian measure commonly associated with VigiBase methods. Different measures and thresholds can produce different alerts.

What the numbers cannot tell you

Spontaneous databases usually lack reliable exposure denominators and contain under-reporting, duplicates, missing data and stimulated reporting. A high value is not an incidence rate; a low value does not prove safety.

Stratification and sensitivity analysis

Age, sex, country, indication, time period, reporter type and comparator selection can materially change results. Sensitivity analyses help test whether a pattern persists after reasonable alternative assumptions.

From statistic to medical review

Review case narratives, chronology, coding, duplicates, outcomes, alternative causes and product role. Compare with labels, literature, class effects, epidemiology and exposure.

  • Confirm the exact drug and event definitions.
  • Inspect the contributing cases.
  • Evaluate reporting context and biases.
  • Look for clinical coherence.
  • Define the safety question requiring validation.

Common interpretation errors

Frequent errors include calling a signal 'positive' solely because a threshold was crossed, comparing scores across databases as if equivalent, ignoring small cell counts and treating absence of disproportionality as absence of association.

AI and statistical signal detection

AI can support semantic event grouping and case review but can amplify bias if trained on the same distorted reporting patterns. Statistical and AI outputs require transparent methods and medical oversight.

How experienced signal teams challenge the evidence

Experienced reviewers deliberately search for evidence that could weaken the apparent association. They examine duplicate cases, stimulated reporting, publicity effects, indication and channeling bias, market growth, country-specific reporting, concomitant treatment and the natural history of the disease. This is not an attempt to dismiss a safety concern. It is how the team prevents an avoidable false conclusion from becoming a regulatory action.

The opposite challenge is equally important. A weak numerical pattern must not be dismissed when the clinical phenotype is distinctive, outcomes are serious, latency is plausible or multiple cases show consistent dechallenge, rechallenge or mechanistic features. Signal science requires the discipline to challenge both amplification and false reassurance.

  • Separate absence of evidence from evidence of absence.
  • Retain contradictory cases and explain their effect.
  • Compare the event at preferred-term, grouped-concept and clinical-syndrome levels.
  • Document why the selected comparator and time window are appropriate.

Operational governance that prevents silent signal failure

A signal tracker should preserve the detected information, validation date, accountable owner, status, evidence reviewed, decisions, actions, deadlines and closure or reopening criteria. Meeting minutes alone are not a signal-management system. The record must show how each conclusion evolved as new information became available.

Interfaces require particular control. Literature, case processing, medical review, epidemiology, regulatory affairs, labelling and risk management may each hold part of the evidence. A strong governance model prevents a relevant finding from remaining trapped inside one function.

  • Define escalation timelines by risk and urgency.
  • Reconcile signal records with PBRERs, RMPs and label changes.
  • Track overdue actions and unresolved evidence gaps.
  • Review closed signals when materially new information emerges.

A practical decision scenario

Consider three reports of the same rare neurological syndrome. The case count is small and no disproportionality threshold is crossed. Yet each case shows compatible latency, extensive alternative-cause exclusion and improvement after withdrawal. A mature system does not close the concern because the statistic is quiet. It defines the clinical concept, retrieves related cases and literature, evaluates class and mechanistic evidence, and records whether the pattern warrants validation. The scenario demonstrates why signal intelligence must combine quantitative surveillance with medically expert review.

Inspection-ready checklist

  • Database version and data cut are recorded.
  • Drug and event definitions are governed.
  • Duplicates and small counts are examined.
  • Comparator and stratification are justified.
  • Case-level review follows the alert.
  • No causality or incidence claim is made from disproportionality alone.

Frequently asked questions

What is PRR?

A measure comparing the proportion of a reported event for one product with the proportion for other products.

What is ROR?

A reporting-odds measure derived from a two-by-two contingency table.

What is IC?

A Bayesian disproportionality measure used in global signal-detection contexts such as VigiBase.

Does a positive score confirm causality?

No. It generates or supports a hypothesis requiring medical and scientific assessment.

Conclusion

Disproportionality is powerful precisely because it is limited: it tells us where reporting looks unusual. The quality of signal management depends on what we do next.

Statistics can point to the door. Only clinical evidence can tell us whether there is a real safety problem behind it.
