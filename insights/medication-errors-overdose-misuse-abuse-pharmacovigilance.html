Medication Errors, Overdose, Misuse and Abuse in Pharmacovigilance

Understand medication error, overdose, misuse and abuse definitions, PV assessment, ICSR reporting, trending and risk minimisation.

TheClinixAI PV Science & Operations 2026-07-234 min read

‘The patient took too much’ can describe an accidental double dose, a prescribing error, intentional misuse, substance abuse or deliberate self-harm. The words overlap. The safety decisions do not.

Accurate classification begins with intent, circumstances and clinical consequence—not a dropdown list.

If the category is wrong, follow-up asks the wrong questions and risk minimisation targets the wrong cause.

Regulatory foundation: GVP Module VI; GVP Module IX; GVP Module V

Keep the definitions distinct

Medication error is an unintentional failure in the treatment process that leads or could lead to harm. Overdose concerns administration above a recommended maximum. Misuse is intentional inappropriate use, while abuse is intentional non-therapeutic use for a perceived effect. Categories can overlap.

Record facts first and assign controlled terms second.

Reconstruct the medication-use pathway

Determine prescribing, transcription, dispensing, preparation, administration, monitoring and storage steps. Ask who detected the problem, whether it reached the patient and what prevented or contributed to harm.

Packaging, labelling, device usability and training may be as important as pharmacology.

Reports with and without adverse events

When an adverse event is present, assess ICSR validity, seriousness, expectedness, causality and regional reporting. When no event occurred, retain relevant error or use information for trending and prevention according to applicable processes.

A near miss is not proof of patient injury, but repeated near misses can reveal a fragile control.

Targeted follow-up

Clarify intended and actual dose, route, frequency, duration, formulation, intent, access, co-ingestants, clinical effects, treatment and outcome. For devices, capture model, configuration and use steps.

Questions about intent must remain neutral and non-judgemental.

Product quality and human factors

Cross-route suspected defects, confusing names, labels, packaging, dosage forms or device malfunctions to quality systems while maintaining PV assessment. Prevent duplicate investigations from fragmenting evidence.

Human-factors evaluation asks why the system made the error possible, not merely who made it.

Signals and risk minimisation

Trend by error type, process step, product, presentation, population, geography and outcome. Risk measures may include label clarification, packaging redesign, training, access controls, dose calculators or device modification.

Effectiveness must be measured after implementation.

Automation risks

Text classification can identify overdose or error language, but negation and context are dangerous: ‘no overdose’ and ‘concern about overdose’ are not events. False negatives should be specifically challenged during validation.

Inspection-ready checklist

  • Intent and circumstances documented.
  • Intended versus actual regimen captured.
  • Error stage identified.
  • Associated events assessed separately.
  • Near misses retained for trending.
  • Quality and device issues cross-routed.
  • Duplicates reconciled.
  • Regional reporting rationale recorded.
  • Root cause informs risk minimisation.
  • Effectiveness is monitored.

Frequently asked questions

Is overdose always intentional?

No. It may be accidental, erroneous or intentional; intent must be established from evidence.

Is every medication error an ICSR?

No. Associated adverse events and applicable regional rules determine case validity and reporting, while error information without harm may still require collection and trending.

Can one report have multiple categories?

Yes. An overdose may result from a medication error or intentional misuse, but each classification should be evidence-based.

What is a near miss?

An error intercepted before reaching the patient or before causing the potential consequence.

Why involve product quality?

Packaging, labelling, formulation or device defects may contribute and require coordinated investigation and correction.

Conclusion

These reports are not only descriptions of what a medicine did. They reveal how medicines are selected, accessed, prepared and used in real systems.

The strongest pharmacovigilance response converts classification into prevention.

Understand the intent, reconstruct the pathway and control the condition that can make the event happen again.
