Lack of Efficacy, Off-Label Use and Occupational Exposure in Pharmacovigilance

A practical PV guide to lack of efficacy, off-label use and occupational exposure assessment, reporting, follow-up and signal evaluation.

By Wany Williams, BDS 2026-07-234 min read

A medicine didn't work. It got used outside its approved indication. A nurse was exposed while preparing a dose. Not one of those sentences, on its own, describes a suspected adverse reaction.

And yet each of them can carry safety information that changes how a case gets handled, how a signal gets evaluated, or what risk management actually does next.

The absence of a conventional adverse event does not mean the absence of a pharmacovigilance question.

Regulatory foundation: GVP Module VI; GVP Module IX; GVP Module V

Why these categories are missed

Intake teams trained to search only for symptoms may exclude treatment failure, use circumstances or workplace exposure. Conversely, automatic case creation may overstate evidence.

A controlled process collects first, then assesses clinical event, validity, reportability and cumulative relevance.

Lack of therapeutic efficacy

Confirm indication, diagnosis, regimen, duration, adherence, administration, storage, interactions, disease course, expected response and objective outcome. Consider resistance, tolerance, product quality and inadequate exposure.

Greater urgency may apply in critical therapy, vaccines or contraceptives, but requirements differ by product and region.

Distinguish failure from disease progression

Temporal worsening during treatment is not automatically an adverse reaction or proof of inefficacy. Medical review should compare the expected natural history, treatment goal and available objective evidence.

Preserve the reporter’s assessment and document the company’s reasoning separately.

Off-label use

Capture the authorised product information applicable at the time and territory, actual indication, population, dose, route and rationale. Off-label use is intentional use outside authorisation; it should not be confused with medication error.

Associated events require normal case assessment. Repeated patterns may inform signals, educational needs or risk-management evaluation.

Occupational exposure

Document occupation, task, route, duration, concentration or dose where known, protective equipment, decontamination, symptoms, treatment and outcome. Distinguish manufacturing, pharmacy, clinical and accidental workplace contexts.

Coordinate with occupational health and product-quality functions while protecting privacy and PV traceability.

Validity and reportability

Do not infer a reaction merely to satisfy a case template. Assess the four minimum ICSR criteria and apply product-, situation- and region-specific reporting rules. Record the rationale for case creation, non-case retention or submission.

Potentially serious consequences require prompt medical triage even before final classification.

Cumulative evaluation

Trend lack-of-efficacy reports by indication and batch, off-label patterns by population and prescriber context, and occupational exposure by task and route. Denominators and exposure context are essential before interpreting rates.

Patterns can trigger signal assessment, quality investigation, labelling change or additional risk minimisation.

Common errors

Frequent errors include treating every progression as inefficacy, coding off-label use as error, losing exposure-only reports, ignoring batch patterns, failing to cross-route quality issues and applying one jurisdiction’s reporting approach globally.

Inspection-ready checklist

  • Indication and expected response documented.
  • Adherence and regimen assessed.
  • Disease progression considered.
  • Off-label rationale and territory captured.
  • Occupational route and controls recorded.
  • Associated events assessed independently.
  • Quality concerns cross-routed.
  • Regional reportability rationale retained.
  • Trends include useful denominators.
  • Medical review addresses uncertainty.

Frequently asked questions

Is lack of efficacy an adverse reaction?

Not automatically. It is a special situation requiring clinical assessment; associated events and applicable rules determine case validity and reporting.

Is off-label use the same as medication error?

No. Off-label use is intentional use outside authorised information; medication error is unintentional.

What is occupational exposure?

Exposure to a medicinal product resulting from professional or non-professional work activities.

Should exposure without symptoms be retained?

Potentially relevant information should be retained and evaluated under applicable procedures even if it does not form a valid ICSR.

Why investigate product quality in lack of efficacy?

Batch, storage, counterfeit, device or manufacturing issues may contribute to apparent treatment failure.

Conclusion

These categories test whether a safety system can actually recognize meaning that goes beyond a symptom list.

Clinical context, product-use context, and exposure context all have to meet in the same place before you can call the decision defensible.

Collect the circumstance, test the explanation and keep the evidence available for the pattern that may only become visible later.
