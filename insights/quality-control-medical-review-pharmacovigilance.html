ICSR Quality, Medical Review & Submission

Quality Control and Medical Review in Pharmacovigilance: Ensuring ICSR Scientific Integrity Before Submission

Learn how ICSR quality control and medical review ensure source fidelity, clinical coherence, correct reportability and submission-ready pharmacovigilance cases.

TheClinixAI PV Science & Operations 2026-07-2316 min read

A serious case arrives late on a Friday. The reporter describes “severe liver injury,” laboratory values are scattered across two follow-up emails, the suspect medicine was stopped, and the patient improved. The case processor enters the information, codes the event and marks the case complete. Every mandatory field appears populated. The submission clock is running.

But the case is not ready.

The chronology shows that the abnormal liver tests began before the suspect medicine was started. The narrative implies improvement after withdrawal, although the source never establishes a true dechallenge. The seriousness criterion was selected from the word “severe,” without evidence of hospitalisation, disability, life-threatening status or another medically important condition. The source documents contain facts that the structured fields do not.

This is where pharmacovigilance quality becomes visible: not when a system accepts the case, but when trained reviewers detect what the system cannot safely decide on its own.

Quality control (QC) and medical review (MR) are often described as final checks. That description is too weak. Together, they are the control layer that converts processed safety data into a scientifically coherent, regulator-ready ICSR. QC protects fidelity, completeness and procedural consistency. Medical review protects clinical meaning, medical reasoning and the defensibility of safety conclusions. Submission converts that governed case into a regulatory message.

This article should be read alongside the principles in EMA Good Pharmacovigilance Practices (GVP), particularly Module VI, and the harmonised standards maintained by the International Council for Harmonisation. Regional rules, company procedures and product-specific obligations must still govern the final operational decision.

What quality actually means in an ICSR

ICSR quality is not equivalent to data-entry completeness. A case can be technically complete and scientifically wrong. It can pass system validation while misrepresenting the source. It can contain a polished narrative that conceals an incorrect chronology. It can be medically plausible but submitted to the wrong authority, under the wrong case classification or outside the applicable timeline.

A defensible ICSR must satisfy four connected dimensions:

  • Source fidelity — the case accurately reflects what was reported, without invention, distortion or silent loss of clinically relevant information.
  • Data integrity — structured fields, codes, dates, products, events and narratives are internally consistent, attributable and traceable.
  • Scientific coherence — the medical interpretation respects chronology, known pharmacology, alternative explanations, clinical course and the limits of available evidence.
  • Regulatory correctness — validity, seriousness, expectedness, reportability, destination and submission timing are determined under the applicable rules.

QC and MR contribute differently to these dimensions. Their responsibilities may overlap, but their purposes should never be collapsed.

If QC is expected to make every medical judgement, procedural review becomes inconsistent and reviewer-dependent. If MR is used to repair basic transcription errors, expensive medical expertise is diverted from genuine clinical reasoning. A mature PV operating model defines decision ownership, escalation rules and evidence requirements while preserving collaboration between the functions.

The correct control sequence

  1. Safety information is received, assessed for validity and assigned a defensible awareness date.
  2. The case processor enters and codes the available information without going beyond the source.
  3. QC compares the processed case with the source and the applicable procedural requirements.
  4. Medical review evaluates the clinical story and medical assessments, including uncertainties and alternative explanations.
  5. Any material findings are corrected, documented and, where necessary, returned through a governed rework loop.
  6. Reportability and submission readiness are confirmed for each applicable market and reporting pathway.
  7. The E2B message is transmitted, the acknowledgement is evaluated and any technical or business rejection is resolved.

The sequence may be configured differently across organisations, especially for non-serious cases, high-volume consumer reports or follow-ups. But the control objectives cannot disappear merely because the workflow is accelerated or automated.

What an ICSR quality-control review must examine

A robust QC review is risk-based, source-driven and sufficiently independent. It does not mean rereading the case casually. It means testing whether the processed record can survive reconstruction by another competent reviewer.

1. Case identity, receipt and audit trail

The reviewer should confirm the case identifier, source type, initial and follow-up classification, receipt dates, awareness date, version history and linkage to the originating communication. Day zero must be supported by evidence, not selected merely because it is convenient for the workflow.

Every source item should be attributable and chronologically ordered. Email bodies, attachments, call records, literature documents, partner transmissions and translations should not become disconnected fragments. If the organisation cannot reconstruct what was known, when it was known and what changed, the case is not inspection-ready.

2. Validity and reporter information

QC should verify that the minimum case criteria remain supported after all follow-up information is considered: an identifiable patient, an identifiable reporter, a suspect medicinal product and a suspected adverse reaction or reportable safety circumstance, as defined by the applicable framework.

Identifiable does not mean that full personal information must be available. It means that sufficient characteristics exist to support the existence of a real patient or reporter and to avoid obvious duplication. Contactability, qualification, country and primary-source status should be represented exactly as supported.

3. Patient, medical history and exposure

Age, sex, weight, pregnancy status, relevant history, indications, concomitant conditions and risk factors should be checked against the source. Exposure dates, dose, route, frequency, formulation, batch information and therapy action must remain aligned with the reported chronology.

Unknown is not the same as not applicable. An estimated date is not the same as an exact date. A calculated age is not the same as a reported age. These distinctions matter because structured data may later support signal detection, aggregate analysis and regulatory decisions.

4. Product and event coding

Medical concepts should be encoded using the current governed version of MedDRA, and medicinal-product coding should follow the organisation’s controlled product dictionary and applicable WHO Drug Global resources. Coding must represent the documented concept; it should not manufacture a diagnosis from symptoms unless the source and coding conventions support it.

QC should assess verbatim-to-code consistency, specificity, hierarchy, duplicate event coding, product role, indication coding and version alignment. When a reporter states “rash and facial swelling,” coding only “rash” loses information. Coding “anaphylaxis” may over-interpret it. The correct outcome depends on the source, follow-up and medically governed coding practice.

5. Seriousness and outcome consistency

The seriousness criteria must be supported by documented facts or a medically justified assessment. Severity describes intensity; seriousness is a regulatory classification based on outcome or intervention criteria. A severe headache may be non-serious. A mild-appearing event can still be serious if it results in hospitalisation or represents a medically important condition.

Event outcome, patient outcome, hospitalisation dates, interventions and seriousness criteria must agree across the structured fields and narrative. QC should identify contradictions; MR should resolve medically complex interpretations.

6. Narrative reconstruction

The narrative is not a decorative summary. It is the human-readable reconstruction of the case. It should establish source, patient context, exposure, event chronology, investigations, treatment, action taken, outcome, reporter assessment, company assessment and relevant follow-up—without repetition or speculation.

A good narrative lets an independent reader understand the case without searching through every field. A defensible narrative also makes uncertainty visible. If dates conflict, the narrative should not silently choose one. If causality was not provided, it should not imply that the reporter assessed the event as related.

7. Cross-field and source-to-case consistency

The strongest QC findings frequently arise between fields rather than within them: an event onset preceding exposure; a positive dechallenge selected while the medicine continued; a recovered outcome while follow-up describes persistent symptoms; an unlisted event marked expected; or an autopsy report attached to a case whose patient outcome remains “unknown.”

A field can be correct in isolation and still make the case wrong when read as part of the clinical story.

What medical review must determine

Medical review begins where mechanical consistency ends. The medical reviewer should not merely approve selections already made by the processor. The reviewer must determine whether the case interpretation is clinically credible, appropriately cautious and supported by the evidence available at that point in time.

Clinical coherence and chronology

The reviewer should reconstruct the temporal relationship between exposure, event onset, dose changes, withdrawal, treatment, investigations and recovery. Chronology does not prove causality, but causality cannot be responsibly assessed without it.

The central question is not simply whether the event occurred after the medicine. It is whether the overall pattern—including biological plausibility, latency, dechallenge, rechallenge, competing causes and background risk—supports or weakens a causal association.

Diagnosis and medical concept selection

A diagnosis may be explicitly reported, medically confirmed, provisionally suspected or inferred from a compatible pattern. Those states are not interchangeable. A reviewer should preserve the source diagnosis while determining whether symptoms, tests and clinical course support the way the case has been represented.

Medical review should also identify when a more important concept is hidden within fragmented data: liver-test abnormalities suggesting potential drug-induced liver injury; mucosal involvement and blistering suggesting a severe cutaneous reaction; or hypotension, bronchospasm and treatment with adrenaline indicating a potentially serious hypersensitivity reaction. The reviewer may request follow-up or refine coding under governed conventions, but must not convert possibility into fact.

Seriousness and medical importance

Seriousness assessment should follow the applicable regulatory framework and controlled medical criteria. Organisations commonly use the EMA Important Medical Events terms list as one decision-support input; it does not eliminate case-specific medical judgement.

A medically important event may be serious even when it did not produce death, immediate life threat, hospitalisation, disability or congenital anomaly, if medical or surgical intervention was required to prevent such an outcome. Conversely, presence on a reference list should not become a substitute for reading the case.

Expectedness and reference safety information

Expectedness must be assessed against the correct, effective reference document for the product, market and reporting context. The event term, nature, specificity, severity and outcome described in the reference safety information matter. A broadly related label concept does not automatically make every clinical presentation expected.

Listedness, expectedness and reportability are connected but not identical across all contexts. The reviewer must know which reference document controls the particular decision and which regional rules apply. Using an obsolete reference version can change an expedited-reporting outcome.

Causality and alternative explanations

Causality is not a confidence score generated from chronology alone. The review should consider temporal compatibility, pharmacological plausibility, known class effects, dose-response information, dechallenge, rechallenge, concomitant medicines, underlying disease, infection, procedures and other credible explanations.

Reporter causality and company causality should remain distinguishable. A company may disagree with a reporter, but the reporter’s assessment must not be overwritten or obscured. Likewise, absence of an explicit reporter assessment should not be converted into “not related.”

Follow-up as a medical decision

Follow-up should be driven by information that can materially change validity, seriousness, causality, expectedness, outcome, case understanding or regulatory action. A generic questionnaire sent to every reporter may produce activity without improving evidence.

  • For hepatic events: baseline and serial laboratory values, competing causes, imaging, alcohol history, viral testing, action taken and recovery.
  • For pregnancy: exposure timing, gestational age, prenatal testing, pregnancy outcome and infant follow-up where applicable.
  • For death: date and cause of death, relationship to the event, autopsy information and relevant terminal clinical course.
  • For medication error or overdose: circumstances, actual exposure, clinical consequences, intervention and preventability information.
  • For lack of efficacy involving critical therapy: product use, adherence, storage, disease course and clinical consequences.

From review findings to governed rework

A finding is useful only when it is classified, corrected and prevented from recurring. Organisations should define critical, major and minor findings based on potential impact, not reviewer preference.

Rework should preserve the audit trail. The original entry, reviewer finding, correction, responsible person and completion time should be reconstructable. Repeated findings should feed training, procedure improvement, system controls and risk-based sampling—not remain isolated reviewer comments.

Risk-based QC without lowering the quality standard

Not every case requires the same intensity of review, but every case requires a controlled standard. A risk-based model can use case characteristics to determine review depth while protecting high-risk decisions.

  • Prioritise fatal, life-threatening, paediatric, pregnancy, abuse, medication-error, product-quality and other medically significant cases.
  • Increase review depth for new products, new indications, emerging safety issues and jurisdictions with complex reporting rules.
  • Use processor performance, error history and case complexity to inform sampling—without creating blind spots.
  • Retain mandatory review points for validity, seriousness, day zero, expectedness, reportability and submission destination.
  • Periodically test the sampling model against fully reviewed control sets to detect false assurance.

A low QC sampling rate is not a quality achievement by itself. It is defensible only when supported by measured performance, stable processes, effective controls and evidence that critical errors are not being missed.

Submission readiness: the final case-level gate

Electronic transmission requires more than a clinically sound case. The message must comply with the applicable ICH E2B(R3) ICSR specification and related files and the receiving authority’s regional implementation rules. For the EU, organisations should consult the EMA EudraVigilance electronic reporting guidance.

Before transmission, the organisation should confirm:

  • The case is valid under the applicable framework and the version is correctly classified as initial, follow-up, amendment or nullification.
  • Seriousness, expectedness and reportability are aligned with the product, territory and reporting context.
  • The correct sender, receiver, message type, case identifiers and worldwide unique case identifier are used.
  • Mandatory E2B elements and regional data rules are satisfied without populating unsupported information.
  • Submission destinations and timelines reflect licences, studies, partner agreements and local obligations.
  • Gateway acknowledgements are monitored, technical and business errors are evaluated, and rejected messages are corrected and retransmitted under controlled procedures.

A transmitted case is not necessarily a successfully submitted case. Regulatory compliance is not demonstrated until the organisation confirms the receiving system’s acknowledgement and resolves any rejection or error that prevents acceptance.

Where automation and AI can—and cannot—help

Automation can compare dates, detect missing fields, identify contradictions, check dictionary versions, validate E2B syntax, flag duplicate narratives and route complex cases. AI can assist with source summarisation, chronology extraction, coding suggestions, narrative drafting and identification of potential medical-review questions.

But assistance must not be confused with accountability. An AI system may produce a clinically fluent explanation that is unsupported by the source. It may interpret a temporal sequence as causality, confuse severity with seriousness or select a diagnosis from incomplete symptoms. Fluency can make an error harder—not easier—to detect.

The safe design principle is simple: automate evidence retrieval and consistency checking aggressively; govern medical conclusions conservatively.

Any automated control should have a defined context of use, validated performance, version governance, traceable inputs and outputs, exception handling and human escalation. False-negative risk deserves particular attention because a system that silently misses a serious or reportable feature may create stronger operational harm than one that produces a visible false alert.

Inspection-ready evidence of quality

An inspection-ready PV system should be able to demonstrate not only that cases were processed, but that the pharmacovigilance system was governed under an effective quality system. EMA GVP Module I provides the EU framework for pharmacovigilance systems and their quality systems, while GVP Module VI addresses collection, management and submission of suspected adverse-reaction reports.

Inspectors and auditors may reasonably expect the organisation to reconstruct:

  • Who processed, quality-checked, medically reviewed and approved the case—and when.
  • Which source documents and translations supported each material decision.
  • Which SOP, work instruction, coding convention, reference safety information and regulatory rule were effective.
  • What findings were raised, how they were corrected and whether the submission outcome changed.
  • How late cases, rejected messages, overrides and deviations were investigated.
  • How quality metrics led to corrective and preventive action, training or system improvement.

A practical ICSR QC and medical-review checklist

  • Case receipt, awareness date and source chronology are traceable.
  • Minimum validity criteria are supported and correctly represented.
  • Patient, reporter, product, event and exposure data match the source.
  • Medical history, concomitant therapy and relevant investigations are complete to the extent available.
  • MedDRA and product coding accurately represent the documented information.
  • Seriousness is supported and not confused with severity.
  • Expectedness uses the correct effective reference safety information.
  • Reporter and company causality assessments are distinct and evidence-based.
  • Narrative, structured fields and source documents tell the same clinical story.
  • Follow-up questions are medically targeted and proportionate.
  • Reportability, destination, timeline and case version are correct.
  • E2B and regional validation rules are satisfied.
  • Acknowledgement monitoring and rejection handling are operational.
  • All review decisions, corrections and overrides are auditable.

The case at the beginning: what disciplined review changes

Return to the liver case. A superficial workflow would submit a serious, positive-dechallenge case because the database fields appear complete. Disciplined QC identifies that the event chronology does not match the source and that “severe” was incorrectly treated as a seriousness criterion. Medical review determines that liver-test abnormalities pre-dated exposure, the reported improvement cannot establish a drug dechallenge and clinically relevant alternative explanations require follow-up.

The purpose of review is not to downgrade the case. It is to represent it honestly.

That distinction protects the patient, the reporter, the company and the integrity of the safety database. It also protects every downstream activity—from expedited reporting and aggregate analysis to signal detection and benefit–risk evaluation—from being built on distorted evidence.

Patient safety is not protected by moving cases faster through a workflow. It is protected by ensuring that every case carries the correct evidence, the correct interpretation and the correct regulatory action.

Frequently asked questions

What is ICSR quality control in pharmacovigilance?

ICSR QC is the structured verification that a processed case accurately represents its source, follows controlled procedures and remains complete, consistent, traceable and regulator-ready. It includes source-to-field comparison, coding checks, chronology review, narrative consistency and confirmation of critical regulatory data.

Is medical review mandatory for every ICSR?

The exact workflow depends on applicable regulations, company procedures, case type and risk model. However, medical accountability must be defined for assessments that require clinical judgement. High-risk and medically complex cases should not depend solely on administrative or automated review.

Can the same person perform case processing and QC?

Organisational models vary, but independence should be sufficient to control confirmation bias and detect errors objectively. Where staffing requires combined responsibilities, compensating controls, clear role separation and documented oversight become important.

What is the difference between QC and medical review?

QC primarily tests fidelity, completeness, consistency and procedural compliance. Medical review evaluates clinical coherence, diagnosis, seriousness, causality, expectedness, alternative explanations and medically meaningful follow-up. Both are connected, but neither replaces the other.

Does passing E2B validation mean the case is medically correct?

No. E2B validation confirms that a message meets technical or business rules. A technically valid message can still contain incorrect chronology, coding, seriousness, causality or medical interpretation.

Can AI perform ICSR quality control?

AI can support extraction, comparison, contradiction detection, coding suggestions and review prioritisation. It should operate within a validated context of use, with traceable evidence and human accountability for consequential medical and regulatory decisions.

Conclusion

A reliable pharmacovigilance system does not treat QC and medical review as obstacles placed between processing and submission. They are the mechanisms through which the organisation proves that speed has not replaced accuracy, completeness has not replaced meaning and automation has not replaced accountability.

The strongest case is not the one with the most populated fields. It is the one whose facts can be traced, whose uncertainties are visible, whose medical reasoning can be defended and whose regulatory action is correct.

That is the standard TheClinixAI believes safety technology must support: evidence before inference, governance before automation and patient safety before workflow convenience.
