Root Cause Analysis and CAPA in Pharmacovigilance

A practical guide to PV problem statements, containment, impact assessment, root-cause methods, CAPA design and inspection-ready closure.

By Divya Jagdish, B.Pharm 2026-07-234 min read

I've sat in this exact closure meeting more times than I can count. A case processor missed a reporting deadline. Someone writes "human error" in the root cause field. Retraining gets assigned. The CAPA closes. Everyone moves on.

Then six months later it happens again, on a different case, with a different processor, and nobody connects the two — because the first investigation never got past the surface. What actually happened the first time: the processor was juggling two mailboxes that weren't linked, the workload alert had quietly been switched off weeks earlier, and nobody had ever written down what "weekend coverage" was supposed to look like. Writing "human error" on the form didn't fix any of that. It just closed the file.

Human error is where the failure showed up. Root cause is why the system let it happen — and why nobody caught it before it reached a patient's case.

Begin with a precise problem statement

Write down exactly what happened — the requirement it breached, the time period, which process and which population were affected, what the actual and potential impact was, and how you found out about it in the first place. Don't put a cause in this box yet, even if you think you already know it.

I've watched this go wrong more than once: someone writes a vague problem statement to save time, and three weeks later the investigation is just as vague, because nobody gave it anything precise to chase.

Contain before completing the investigation

Before you go digging for the "why," protect what's live right now. Find every affected record. Submit or correct any cases that need it. Lock down the data. If a process is clearly unreliable, pause it. Put a temporary review step in place, and escalate anything serious immediately. Then write down why you're confident the interim controls are actually enough — don't just say they are.

The trap here is letting containment quietly become the permanent fix, because it feels like the problem's handled. It isn't, until the root cause is found and closed.

Assess scope and impact

Find the first time this happened, not just the one you caught. Find the last. Map every product, country, case, submission, signal, report, partner and system it touched — and don't stop at the sample that got flagged. Go looking for more.

Then weigh the impact properly: patient safety first, then the scientific, regulatory, data-integrity and inspection angles.

Collect evidence, not opinions

Pull the timestamps. Pull the audit trails. Look at workload, emails, agreements, training records, procedures, system configuration, recent changes, and any past deviations that smell similar. Piece together what actually happened, in order, before you even pick which analysis method to use.

When you interview people, ask them to walk you through the work as they actually did it. You're not looking for a confession — you're looking for the gap between the SOP and reality.

Root-cause methods

Five Whys is good for a fairly linear chain of cause and effect. Fishbone analysis helps when you need to lay out people, process, technology, environment, materials and management side by side. Barrier analysis asks a sharper question: which controls — preventive and detective — should have caught this, and why didn't they? Some of the messier PV failures need more than one of these run together.

None of these tools does the thinking for you. A method is only as good as the evidence backing each link in the chain.

Differentiate root, contributing and escape causes

The root cause is what actually created the failure. Contributing factors are what made it more likely, or made it worse when it happened. The escape cause is a separate question entirely: why didn't anything already in place catch it?

Fix the root without asking that last question, and you can end up just as blind to the next one as you were to this one.

Design actions against causes

Corrective actions need to directly break a causal link you've actually verified — not one you assumed. Preventive actions should look sideways, at where else the same risk could be sitting. For each one, name an owner, a real deliverable, a due date, any dependency, an interim control if needed, and — critically — how you'll know it worked.

I'd rather see two well-aimed actions than ten vague ones. Ten weak actions just means more paperwork to track, not a stronger system.

Common weak CAPAs

"Retrain the team" again. Revise the whole SOP without touching the actual gap. Send a reminder email. Add "monitoring." None of these mean much unless you can trace them back to a specific, verified cause. Even a shiny new system automation is weak if nobody's defined the requirements, the exception handling, or who owns it once it's live.

Before you sign off on any action, ask yourself plainly: which cause does this remove, and what evidence will show me it actually worked?

Closure and traceability

Everything needs to trace: the problem, the evidence, the impact, the cause, the action, and how you'll check effectiveness. If the cause or the action changes partway through, write down why — and go back and reconsider the impact assessment in light of it.

Whoever signs off on closure should be independent enough to actually push back if it looks premature. That's not a formality — it's the whole point of having a separate approver.

Inspection-ready checklist

  • Problem statement is factual.
  • Immediate containment documented.
  • Scope extends beyond the initial sample.
  • Patient and regulatory impact assessed.
  • Event timeline reconstructed.
  • Evidence supports causal links.
  • Root, contributing and escape causes separated.
  • Actions map directly to causes.
  • Owners and due dates are realistic.
  • Effectiveness criteria defined before closure.

Frequently asked questions

What is root cause analysis in PV?

A structured, evidence-based investigation of why a PV failure occurred and why controls did not prevent or detect it.

Is human error a root cause?

Usually not by itself. The investigation should examine why the error was likely, possible and undetected.

What is the difference between corrective and preventive action?

Corrective action addresses the cause of an observed issue; preventive action addresses similar potential failures elsewhere.

Should every deviation require CAPA?

Not necessarily. The response should be risk-based, but the rationale and impact assessment should be documented.

Can training be an effective CAPA?

Yes, when competence is a verified cause and the training’s effectiveness is objectively tested.

Conclusion

Root-cause analysis was never really about finding the person standing closest to the failure when it happened. It's about understanding the system around them — what shaped the decision they made, what let the error through, and what should have caught it but didn't.

A CAPA worth its name changes that system in a way you can actually go back and measure later.

Before you ask who made the mistake, ask why the process couldn't absorb one safely in the first place. That's the question that actually prevents the next one.
