Root Cause Analysis and CAPA in Pharmacovigilance

A practical guide to PV problem statements, containment, impact assessment, root-cause methods, CAPA design and inspection-ready closure.

TheClinixAI PV Science & Operations 2026-07-234 min read

A case processor missed a reporting deadline. ‘Human error’ is recorded as the root cause. Retraining is assigned. The CAPA closes.

But the processor was working from two unconnected mailboxes, the workload alert had been disabled and the procedure did not define weekend coverage. Calling the failure human error protected the system from investigation.

Human error is where the failure appeared. Root cause is why the system made that failure possible and allowed it to escape.

Regulatory foundation: GVP Module I; GVP Module IV; GVP Module II; MHRA inspection metrics

Begin with a precise problem statement

Define what occurred, requirement, period, process, population, actual and potential impact, and how the issue was detected. Avoid assigning cause in the problem statement.

A vague statement produces a vague investigation.

Contain before completing the investigation

Protect current obligations: identify affected records, submit or correct cases, secure data, pause an unreliable process, establish temporary review and escalate serious risk. Document why interim controls are adequate.

Containment should not become a permanent workaround.

Assess scope and impact

Determine first occurrence, last occurrence, affected products, countries, cases, submissions, signals, reports, partners and systems. Test beyond the detected sample.

Assess patient-safety, scientific, regulatory, data-integrity and inspection impact.

Collect evidence, not opinions

Review timestamps, audit trails, workload, emails, agreements, training, procedures, configuration, changes, previous deviations and interviews. Reconstruct the event sequence before selecting a tool.

Interview questions should explore the work as performed, not demand confession.

Root-cause methods

Five Whys can explore a linear cause; fishbone analysis can structure people, process, technology, environment, materials and management factors; barrier analysis asks which preventive and detective controls failed. Complex PV events may require several methods.

The method is useful only when evidence supports each causal link.

Differentiate root, contributing and escape causes

Root cause creates the failure. Contributing factors increase likelihood or impact. Escape cause explains why existing controls did not detect it.

Correcting only the root without strengthening detection may leave the organisation blind to recurrence.

Design actions against causes

Corrective actions should directly break verified causal pathways. Preventive actions should address comparable risk elsewhere. Define owner, deliverable, due date, dependency, interim control and success criterion.

Avoid action inflation: ten weak actions create more administration without stronger control.

Common weak CAPAs

Repeated training, broad SOP revision, reminder emails and ‘monitoring’ are weak when not tied to cause. System automation is also weak if requirements, exception handling and ownership remain unclear.

Every action should answer: which cause will this remove, and what evidence will prove it?

Closure and traceability

Keep problem, evidence, impact, cause, action and effectiveness logically connected. Changes in cause or action require documented rationale and renewed impact consideration.

Closure approval should be independent enough to challenge premature completion.

Inspection-ready checklist

  • Problem statement is factual.
  • Immediate containment documented.
  • Scope extends beyond the initial sample.
  • Patient and regulatory impact assessed.
  • Event timeline reconstructed.
  • Evidence supports causal links.
  • Root, contributing and escape causes separated.
  • Actions map directly to causes.
  • Owners and due dates are realistic.
  • Effectiveness criteria defined before closure.

Frequently asked questions

What is root cause analysis in PV?

A structured, evidence-based investigation of why a PV failure occurred and why controls did not prevent or detect it.

Is human error a root cause?

Usually not by itself. The investigation should examine why the error was likely, possible and undetected.

What is the difference between corrective and preventive action?

Corrective action addresses the cause of an observed issue; preventive action addresses similar potential failures elsewhere.

Should every deviation require CAPA?

Not necessarily. The response should be risk-based, but the rationale and impact assessment should be documented.

Can training be an effective CAPA?

Yes, when competence is a verified cause and the training’s effectiveness is objectively tested.

Conclusion

Root-cause analysis is not an exercise in finding the person closest to the failure. It is an examination of the system that shaped the decision, permitted the error and failed to detect it.

A strong CAPA changes that system in a way that can be measured.

Do not ask who made the mistake until you have asked why the process could not safely absorb one.
