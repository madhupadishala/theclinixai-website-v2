DSUR Preparation in Pharmacovigilance: Development Safety Update Report Explained

A complete guide to DSUR preparation, DIBD, annual safety data, SUSAR analysis, trial risk and ICH E2F compliance.

By Madhu Padishala 2026-07-234 min read

I've watched a development programme rack up thousands of data points and still leave the one question that actually matters unanswered: is it still ethically and medically justified to keep exposing participants?

That's the question the DSUR exists to answer, periodically and cumulatively, whether the answer is comfortable or not.

The DSUR is not an annual archive of SUSARs. It is the sponsor’s integrated safety judgement on the investigational drug and the people still being exposed.

Regulatory foundation: ICH E2F; ICH E2A; ICH E2C(R2)

Purpose and scope

ICH E2F defines a common annual format for safety reporting on investigational drugs. The DSUR integrates worldwide development information over the reporting period and cumulative programme.

Development international birth date and data lock

Govern the DIBD, reporting period, product scope, combinations and reference safety information. Misalignment across trials creates incomplete or duplicated analysis.

Core evidence

Include worldwide authorisation status, actions for safety reasons, trial inventory, exposure, serious adverse reactions, important findings, non-clinical information, literature, signals and changes to the investigator’s brochure.

Cumulative safety evaluation

The sponsor should analyse patterns across trials, doses, routes, populations and indications. Small numbers, unblinding limits and comparator context must be explicit.

Risks and trial continuation

New risks should be assessed for impact on informed consent, protocol, monitoring, investigator communication, RSI, recruitment or trial continuation.

DSUR QC and governance

Reconcile clinical, safety, regulatory and non-clinical sources. Ensure deaths, withdrawals, important deviations and actions are consistent across sections and appendices.

The scientific questions every aggregate team must answer

I push teams to interrogate the reporting period, not just describe it. What changed in exposure? Which events got more common or shifted clinically? Did a new study change how we understand a risk or benefit? Did a signal assessment change the safety specification? Did regulatory action in one country create implications somewhere else? Did the risk-minimisation data actually show the intervention worked — or that it quietly failed?

A useful report distinguishes new information from new interpretation. Sometimes no new event is identified, yet cumulative evidence changes severity, frequency, susceptible population, preventability or clinical management. That change can be as important as discovery of a new reaction.

  • Explain important interval changes against cumulative context.
  • State limitations of exposure and case ascertainment.
  • Reconcile signal conclusions with RMP and product information.
  • Identify concrete actions arising from the evaluation.

Authoring governance and cross-functional accountability

Aggregate reports are assembled across safety operations, medical review, biostatistics, epidemiology, clinical development, regulatory affairs, labelling and risk management. The author should not be forced to discover at final review that these functions used different data cuts or product scopes.

A controlled source matrix should define each input, system of record, owner, cut-off, format, reconciliation requirement and approval. Review comments should be resolved with documented rationale, especially when they change risk classification or benefit–risk conclusions.

  • Freeze governed datasets before final medical interpretation.
  • Use one controlled source for repeated numbers.
  • Record material judgement changes between drafts.
  • Link conclusions to follow-up actions and accountable owners.

A practical decision scenario

Consider a reporting period in which spontaneous case counts for a known risk rise by 60%. The increase appears alarming until exposure data show a major market expansion and a communication campaign that stimulated reporting. At the same time, the proportion of cases involving hospitalisation has increased in one vulnerable population. A strong aggregate report does not choose between 'more reports' and 'no change.' It separates reporting volume from clinical pattern, evaluates population-specific severity and determines whether targeted risk action is required.

Inspection-ready checklist

  • DIBD and scope are governed.
  • Trial inventory and exposure reconcile.
  • SUSAR and death data are complete.
  • RSI version is correct.
  • Signals and actions are current.
  • Participant-protection implications are explicit.
  • Approvals and submissions are traceable.

Frequently asked questions

What is a DSUR?

An annual cumulative safety report for investigational drugs during clinical development.

Is a DSUR the same as a PBRER?

No. DSUR focuses on development safety and trial participants; PBRER focuses on marketed-product benefit–risk.

What is the DIBD?

The development international birth date used to establish the annual reporting cycle.

Does the DSUR replace expedited SUSAR reporting?

No. Periodic reporting and expedited reporting serve different obligations.

Conclusion

A DSUR should tell you plainly whether the evidence still supports continuing development — and what needs to change to protect participants if the safety profile has moved.

If a DSUR doesn't change how the programme gets monitored, communicated, or governed, it's stopped being patient protection. It's just paperwork with a compliance stamp on it.
