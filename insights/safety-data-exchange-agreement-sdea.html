PV Agreements & Partner Governance

Safety Data Exchange Agreement: Essential Clauses, Timelines and Responsibilities

Learn essential SDEA clauses for scope, day zero, case transfer, submissions, literature, reconciliation, audit and termination.

By Dr. Surya, Doctorate in Pharmacy 2026-07-234 min read

I've reviewed SDEAs that both parties signed in good faith and that still broke down on the very first serious case. One defined a two-day transfer timeline but never defined what day zero actually was. Another assigned submission responsibility without naming which countries it covered. A third required reconciliation but never said what evidence would actually prove the exchange was complete.

A signature creates an agreement. It's the operational detail underneath it that creates an actual control.

A safety agreement should have already answered the hard question before a case ever arrives — not while the reporting clock is running and someone's scrambling to interpret a clause.

Regulatory foundation: GVP Module I; GVP Module II; GVP Module VI; EMA PV system Q&A

Purpose and scope

Name the parties precisely — legal entities, not just brand names — along with the products, formulations, territories, lifecycle status, effective date and the nature of the relationship. Say plainly whether the agreement covers marketed products, clinical trials, named-patient use, or transitional activity following a divestment or acquisition. Vagueness here surfaces later, usually at the worst possible time.

Definitions that prevent disputes

Every term that matters needs a real definition: adverse event, adverse reaction, special situation, valid case, awareness, initial information, follow-up, seriousness, day zero, business day, calendar day. Use the regulatory meaning of each term, not whatever shorthand happened to be convenient when the commercial deal was drafted.

Case collection and transfer

Spell out intake routes, the minimum dataset required for transfer, how source documents are handled, language, privacy, format, who's sending, who's receiving, how acknowledgement works, and what happens when a transfer fails. In my experience, the agreements that hold up are the ones that address incomplete reports and special situations directly, rather than assuming every case will arrive fully validated.

Follow-up, duplicates and medical information

Decide, in writing, who contacts the original reporter, who owns follow-up questions, how a suspected duplicate gets flagged between parties, and how additional information is versioned as it arrives. When both parties independently follow up with the same reporter, you don't just create inefficiency — you confuse the reporter and end up with fragmented, inconsistent evidence.

Regulatory submission

Assign submission responsibility by product, by country, and by case category — not as a blanket statement. Define how reportability gets assessed, how the message is generated, who monitors the gateway, how acknowledgement and rejection are handled, how corrections happen, and how proof of acceptance is retained.

One party submitting globally is not the same as one party covering every local obligation. I've seen that assumption cause real gaps.

Literature and other sources

Assign global and local literature monitoring clearly — search frequency, which databases, local-language journals, translation handling, duplicate management, and who creates the case once something relevant turns up. Where they apply, also cover medical information queries, product complaints, digital media monitoring, market research and patient-support programmes.

Signals, aggregate reports and risk management

Define who has access to case-level data for signal work, how a signal gets notified between parties, who validates and assesses it, how communication with health authorities is coordinated, who contributes to PBRER/PSUR or DSUR sections, how data-lock points are agreed, and how RMP changes and urgent safety issues get escalated.

Reconciliation and compliance

State the frequency, the dataset in scope, what counts as evidence, how discrepancies are classified, how late-case impact is assessed, closure timelines, and the escalation path. Your metrics should reflect quality and acceptance rates — not simply how many cases were exchanged, which tells you very little on its own.

Audit, inspection and CAPA

Define audit rights, inspection support obligations, response timelines, access to records, how findings get communicated between parties, CAPA ownership, and how effectiveness evidence is shared. Confidentiality provisions cannot be used to prevent a regulated party from meeting its own inspection obligations — that's a line I hold firmly in every agreement I review.

Change, continuity and termination

Cover contact updates, how regulatory change is communicated, system changes on either side, business continuity and disaster recovery, data migration, divestment scenarios, termination mechanics, record retention, and what happens to open obligations when the relationship ends.

Inspection-ready checklist

  • Scope names products, territories and entities.
  • Day zero and transfer timelines are defined.
  • Initial and follow-up formats are controlled.
  • Submission destinations and acknowledgements are assigned.
  • Literature and special situations are covered.
  • Signals and aggregate responsibilities are included.
  • Reconciliation method and evidence are defined.
  • Audit, CAPA and inspection support are enforceable.
  • Termination preserves records and open work.

Frequently asked questions

What is an SDEA?

A safety data exchange agreement defines how two or more parties share and govern pharmacovigilance information and responsibilities.

What is the most important SDEA clause?

No single clause is sufficient; scope, awareness, timelines, submission ownership, reconciliation and oversight must work together.

Should incomplete cases be exchanged?

The agreement should reflect applicable obligations and generally avoid delaying potentially relevant safety information while waiting for completeness.

Who owns follow-up?

The agreement should assign ownership and coordination to prevent duplicate contact and fragmented evidence.

What happens after termination?

Open cases, submissions, signals, records, retention and inspection support must transition through a controlled plan.

Conclusion

An SDEA has done its job when the people operating under it never have to interpret it under pressure. The roles, the clocks, the formats and the escalation paths should already be understood, already tested, and already measurable well before a serious case shows up.

Ambiguity written into an SDEA doesn't stay theoretical for long. It becomes delay written directly into your safety process.
