Special Situations in Pharmacovigilance: A Complete Guide to Collection, Assessment and Reporting

A complete guide to pregnancy, medication errors, overdose, misuse, abuse, off-label use, lack of efficacy and occupational exposure in PV.

TheClinixAI PV Science & Operations 2026-07-235 min read

A call centre receives a report that a pregnant patient used a medicine. No adverse event is mentioned. A second report describes an overdose without symptoms. A third describes treatment failure in a life-threatening infection.

If all three are forced into the same workflow, the system will either over-report, under-collect or lose the clinical context that makes the information meaningful.

Special situations demand a controlled distinction between safety-information collection, ICSR validity, regulatory reportability and cumulative safety evaluation.

The category tells us what must be noticed. The evidence tells us what can be concluded. The applicable rule tells us what must be reported.

Regulatory foundation: GVP Module VI; EMA Pregnancy and Breastfeeding GVP; GVP Module IX; GVP Module V

What are special situations in pharmacovigilance?

Special situations are product-use or exposure circumstances that may carry safety relevance even when an adverse reaction is not initially reported. Common categories include pregnancy or breastfeeding exposure, medication error, overdose, misuse, abuse, off-label use, occupational exposure and lack of therapeutic efficacy.

They should enter a governed intake and assessment pathway. That does not mean every report automatically meets the four minimum criteria for a valid ICSR or requires expedited submission.

The four decisions that must remain separate

A scientifically reliable process makes four explicit decisions: whether the information is in scope for collection; whether it forms a valid ICSR; whether regional rules require submission; and whether it contributes to aggregate evaluation, signal management or risk minimisation.

Collapsing these decisions is a frequent source of inconsistent case creation and unreliable compliance metrics.

  • Collect the source faithfully.
  • Classify the special situation.
  • Assess patient, reporter, product and event information.
  • Determine regional reportability and timelines.
  • Retain non-case safety information for appropriate evaluation.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding exposure

Pregnancy exposure requires attention to timing, dose, indication, maternal history, concomitant exposures, gestational age and pregnancy outcome. Follow-up may extend to fetal, neonatal or infant outcomes. Breastfeeding reports require exposure timing, feeding pattern, infant age and relevant maternal and infant events.

Exposure without an adverse outcome can remain important for prospective follow-up, pregnancy registries and cumulative evaluation, even where it is not an expedited ICSR. The EMA’s final 2026 population-specific GVP considerations provide the current European framework.

Medication error, overdose, misuse and abuse

Medication error is generally unintentional; misuse and abuse involve intentional use but differ in purpose and context; overdose concerns administration above the recommended maximum. The original reporter’s facts should be preserved before coding a category.

Reports with associated adverse reactions are assessed under applicable ICSR rules. Reports without an adverse reaction can still reveal confusing packaging, device design, prescribing risk, access patterns or emerging misuse and should not disappear from safety surveillance.

Off-label use and occupational exposure

Off-label use describes intentional use outside authorised product information. It is not itself proof of an adverse reaction, but associated events require assessment. Occupational exposure occurs through work, including manufacturing or clinical handling, and requires documentation of route, duration, protective controls, dose where estimable and clinical consequences.

Lack of therapeutic efficacy

Lack of efficacy requires clinical judgement. Disease progression, incorrect diagnosis, non-adherence, inadequate exposure, interaction, resistance, product quality and true pharmacological failure can produce similar narratives.

The regulatory significance may be greater for vaccines, contraceptives or medicines used in serious or life-threatening conditions. Organisations should apply product- and region-specific rules rather than treating every non-response as either reportable or irrelevant.

A controlled end-to-end workflow

The workflow should begin at first organisational awareness, preserve the verbatim source, assign the correct category and route urgent clinical or product-quality concerns. Case validity, seriousness, expectedness, causality and reportability should then be assessed by qualified roles.

Follow-up questions must be proportionate to the category. Coding, duplicate search, narrative and submission should preserve why the situation matters, while non-valid information remains retrievable for signal and aggregate review.

  • Recognise and date-stamp awareness.
  • Triage urgent medical and quality risks.
  • Classify without inventing facts.
  • Assess validity and reportability.
  • Conduct targeted follow-up.
  • Code and medically review.
  • Submit where required and confirm acceptance.
  • Trend, signal and feed risk management.

Data quality and follow-up

Generic follow-up forms create noise. Pregnancy needs outcome and timing data; medication error needs the point of failure; lack of efficacy needs indication, regimen, adherence and disease course; occupational exposure needs route and protective measures.

Every unanswered question should have a safety purpose. Repeated requests that cannot change assessment burden reporters and rarely improve evidence.

Signals, aggregate reports and risk management

A single report may not be a valid case yet still contribute to a pattern. Recurrent wrong-route errors, clusters of occupational exposure or repeated contraceptive failure can require signal assessment, label change, packaging improvement, educational material or additional pharmacovigilance.

GVP Module IX and GVP Module V connect detection with structured evaluation and risk minimisation.

Automation with accountable human review

Automation can recognise special-situation language, prioritise pregnancy or overdose, propose follow-up questions and identify trends. It should not infer an adverse event, invent exposure details or determine reportability without transparent rules and human accountability.

Validation should test false negatives, ambiguous language, negation, multilingual reports and category overlap.

The inspection test

An inspector may select one exposure report and ask why it was collected, why a case was or was not created, what follow-up occurred, which reporting rule was applied and how the information entered cumulative evaluation.

The defensible answer is a documented decision path—not a statement that the system always treats the category the same way.

Inspection-ready checklist

  • All intake channels recognise special situations.
  • Definitions are controlled and region-aware.
  • Collection is separated from ICSR validity.
  • Reportability decisions cite applicable rules.
  • Category-specific follow-up is used.
  • Pregnancy outcomes are longitudinally tracked.
  • Non-valid reports remain available for evaluation.
  • Quality complaints and device issues are cross-routed.
  • Signals and risk-management feedback are documented.
  • Automation retains human accountability.

Frequently asked questions

Are all special situations reportable ICSRs?

No. They should be collected and assessed, but validity and regulatory submission depend on the available information and applicable regional requirements.

Is pregnancy exposure without an adverse event important?

Yes. It may require prospective follow-up, registry consideration and cumulative evaluation even when it is not an expedited ICSR.

Is off-label use an adverse event?

No. It is a use circumstance. Any associated adverse event or reaction must be assessed separately.

How is misuse different from medication error?

Medication error is unintentional; misuse is generally intentional inappropriate use. Exact definitions should follow the applicable framework.

Can non-valid reports contribute to signals?

Yes. Properly retained non-case information can contribute to trend, signal and risk evaluation.

Conclusion

Special situations are not exceptions to pharmacovigilance discipline. They are the situations in which that discipline is most visible.

A mature system neither reports everything mechanically nor dismisses what is incomplete. It preserves the evidence, applies the correct decision and keeps the information available for the next safety question.

When category, evidence and obligation are kept separate, special situations become scientifically useful instead of operationally confusing.
