PV Audits & CAPA

How to Build a Risk-Based Pharmacovigilance Audit Programme

Learn how to map the PV audit universe, score risk, select audit types, set frequencies, govern deferrals and build a GVP-aligned audit plan.

By Wany Williams, BDS 2026-07-234 min read

An audit calendar can be full and still miss the greatest risk. Internal case processing may be reviewed every year while a high-volume distributor, a new safety database and three local affiliates remain outside scope.

Frequency is not risk coverage. Activity is not assurance.

A risk-based audit programme must explain not only what will be audited—but why something else can safely wait.

Regulatory foundation: GVP Module IV; GVP Module I; GVP Module II; MHRA GPvP guidance

Start with the complete audit universe

Map every process, legal entity, affiliate, partner, vendor, system, product interface and country activity that can affect PV obligations. Include activities outside the formal safety department, such as medical information, complaints, market research and digital channels where relevant.

Unmapped activity cannot be risk-ranked and often becomes the hidden source of inspection findings.

Separate strategy from tactical planning

The audit strategy defines long-term coverage principles, risk methodology and cycle. The tactical plan identifies specific audits, timing, objectives and resources for the period.

Both should be approved, controlled and revised when risk changes.

Risk factors that matter

Consider patient-safety impact, reporting criticality, product profile, exposure, activity volume, jurisdictions, complexity, outsourcing, data interfaces, recent change, performance trends, previous findings, deviations, CAPA and regulatory intelligence.

Risk scoring should support judgement rather than replace it.

Inherent risk and control effectiveness

Inherent risk exists before controls. Residual risk reflects the quality and evidence of those controls. A high-risk process may justify a longer interval only when strong independent evidence supports control effectiveness.

Self-reported green metrics should not automatically lower audit priority.

Selecting audit types

Use system, process, product, affiliate, partner, vendor, computerised-system and for-cause audits according to the question. Integrated audits can examine interfaces, but overly broad scopes may dilute depth.

For-cause audits require clear escalation and confidentiality.

Frequency and cycle

Set intervals according to risk and legal expectations, then define triggers for earlier review: acquisition, new product, system migration, vendor change, late submissions, signal failure, inspection finding or whistleblower information.

Document why low-risk elements are deferred and how they remain monitored.

Resource and auditor competence

Match audit complexity with PV, medical, regulatory, data and technology expertise. Ensure independence, language capability and sufficient time for preparation, fieldwork and reporting.

A plan that exceeds available capacity is not a plan; it is an unmanaged backlog.

Plan changes and governance

Track completion, scope change, cancellation, deferral, emerging risk and overdue reports. Governance should challenge whether the universe remains complete and whether repeated deferrals create unacceptable blind spots.

Maintain history so an inspector can reconstruct audit-planning decisions.

Inspection-ready checklist

  • Complete audit universe approved.
  • Strategy and tactical plan distinguished.
  • Risk factors and scoring documented.
  • Control effectiveness evidenced.
  • Outsourced activities included.
  • Audit types match objectives.
  • Change triggers are defined.
  • Competence and independence assessed.
  • Deferrals include rationale and controls.
  • Plan performance reaches governance.

Frequently asked questions

What is a risk-based PV audit programme?

A structured approach that prioritises audit coverage according to patient-safety, compliance and system risk.

Must every PV activity be audited annually?

Not necessarily. Coverage and frequency should follow applicable requirements and documented risk.

Should vendors be in the audit universe?

Yes, where their activities or interfaces affect the PV system.

What triggers a for-cause audit?

Credible information suggesting significant or systemic failure, such as serious compliance trends, whistleblower information or critical incidents.

Can good KPIs reduce audit frequency?

Potentially, but only when definitions and underlying control evidence are reliable and the wider risk remains acceptable.

Conclusion

The audit programme is the organisation’s map of where assurance is most needed. If that map excludes complex interfaces, outsourced activities or recent change, it will direct auditors toward familiarity rather than risk.

A defensible plan is dynamic, evidence-based and willing to change when the system changes.

Audit where failure can matter most—not simply where auditing is easiest.
