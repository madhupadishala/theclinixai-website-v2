Pregnancy and Breastfeeding Exposure in Pharmacovigilance: Collection, Follow-up and Reporting

Learn pregnancy and breastfeeding exposure collection, prospective follow-up, outcomes, ICSR validity, registries and cumulative safety evaluation.

By Wany Williams, BDS 2026-07-234 min read

A report that just says "exposed during pregnancy" can look thin at first glance. Scientifically, though, it might be the very first entry in a safety record that follows the pregnancy all the way through delivery and infant follow-up.

The goal at intake was never to force an outcome before it's known. It's to preserve a prospective observation while there's still no outcome attached to it.

Pregnancy pharmacovigilance is longitudinal evidence generation—not a one-time case-processing transaction.

Regulatory foundation: EMA Pregnancy and Breastfeeding GVP; EMA Pregnancy Guidance; FDA Pregnancy Exposure Registries; GVP Module VI

Why pregnancy evidence is different

Background rates of miscarriage, congenital anomalies and pregnancy complications complicate causal interpretation. Timing by trimester, disease, concomitant medicines and comparator information matter.

Spontaneous cases can generate signals, while registries and other post-authorisation studies can provide more systematic evidence.

What to collect at first contact

Capture the exposed person, reporter, medicinal product, indication, dose, route, dates, last menstrual period or estimated gestational age, concomitant exposures, medical and obstetric history, and whether an outcome or adverse event is known.

Distinguish maternal exposure, paternal exposure where relevant, in-utero exposure and exposure through breastmilk.

Prospective and retrospective reports

A prospective report is received before the outcome is known and is particularly valuable because knowledge of the outcome has not selected the report. A retrospective report begins after an outcome is known and remains important but has different reporting bias.

The classification and original awareness date should be documented.

Outcome follow-up

Follow-up may include prenatal tests, pregnancy complications, delivery date and mode, gestational age, live birth, fetal loss, termination, congenital anomaly, neonatal condition and infant development where relevant.

Follow-up schedules should match product risk, protocol and applicable guidance without making unsupported clinical promises.

Breastfeeding exposure

Record maternal dose and timing, milk feeding pattern, infant age and prematurity, duration, infant symptoms and other exposures. Maternal and infant information may require linked but clearly distinguished records.

Do not infer infant exposure or causality solely because breastfeeding occurred.

Validity and reporting

Exposure alone and a suspected adverse reaction are not interchangeable. Assess the four minimum ICSR criteria and applicable regional rules. Document why information was submitted, retained for follow-up or included in cumulative evaluation.

Serious fetal or infant outcomes require timely medical review and precise case linkage.

Pregnancy registries and cumulative evidence

Pregnancy exposure registries systematically collect exposed pregnancy outcomes and may include comparator groups. FDA describes them as research studies that improve information on medicines and vaccines used during pregnancy.

Interpretation needs denominators, comparator selection, loss-to-follow-up assessment and recognition of confounding.

Common quality failures

Frequent failures include missing exposure timing, closing a report before outcome, counting mother and infant incorrectly, duplicating registry and spontaneous reports, treating normal outcome as proof of safety and inferring causality from temporal association.

Inspection-ready checklist

  • Exposure timing and gestational age captured.
  • Prospective status documented.
  • Maternal, fetal and infant identities distinguished.
  • Outcome follow-up scheduled.
  • Concomitant exposures collected.
  • Breastfeeding pattern recorded.
  • Case linkage and duplicates controlled.
  • Validity and reportability documented.
  • Registry information reconciled.
  • Cumulative evaluation receives the data.

Frequently asked questions

Is pregnancy exposure automatically a valid ICSR?

No. It is important safety information, but ICSR validity depends on the minimum criteria and whether a suspected reaction or other reportable circumstance is present under applicable rules.

Why are prospective reports valuable?

They are captured before the outcome is known and are less affected by outcome-driven reporting.

How long should follow-up continue?

According to the product, outcome, study or registry protocol, applicable guidance and clinical relevance.

Should maternal and infant events be linked?

Yes, where appropriate, while preserving distinct subjects and clear relationships.

Do normal outcomes prove a medicine is safe in pregnancy?

No. Individual normal outcomes contribute evidence but cannot establish population-level safety.

Conclusion

Pregnancy and breastfeeding surveillance works when that first, incomplete exposure report grows into a traceable, clinically interpretable chain of evidence over time.

How good the final assessment turns out to be really comes down to the questions asked before anyone knew the outcome.

Follow the exposure, preserve the timeline and let cumulative evidence—not assumption—shape the conclusion.
