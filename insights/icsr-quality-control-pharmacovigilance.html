ICSR Quality, Medical Review & Submission

ICSR Quality Control in Pharmacovigilance: A Complete Case-QC Checklist

A complete ICSR QC checklist covering source verification, coding, chronology, seriousness, narratives, reportability and submission readiness.

TheClinixAI PV Science & Operations 2026-07-236 min read

A case can be fully populated, medically plausible and still be wrong. The patient’s age may have been calculated from an uncertain date. The event may be coded correctly but assigned to the wrong product. A hospitalisation may appear in the narrative while seriousness remains non-serious. A submission can pass technical validation while failing the most basic quality test: does the case accurately represent the source?

ICSR quality control is not proofreading. It is controlled reconstruction of the evidence before the case becomes a regulatory record.

This is why mature pharmacovigilance organisations do not define quality by the number of completed fields. They define it by source fidelity, internal consistency, traceability, procedural compliance and the ability of an independent reviewer to defend every material decision.

The regulatory foundation is established through EMA Good Pharmacovigilance Practices, including GVP Module I for the quality system and GVP Module VI for the collection, management and submission of suspected adverse-reaction reports.

What ICSR quality control actually means

ICSR QC is the systematic comparison of the processed safety case against the source documents, governed conventions and applicable procedural requirements. Its purpose is to identify errors or omissions that could affect patient safety, case interpretation, reportability, timelines, transmission or downstream analysis.

QC does not replace case processing and it does not replace medical review. Processing creates the structured record. QC verifies that record. Medical review resolves clinical judgements that require qualified interpretation.

The complete ICSR QC framework

1. Receipt, source and day-zero verification

Confirm every source item, receipt date, awareness date, routing record and follow-up version. Day zero must be supported by when the organisation first possessed sufficient information under the applicable framework—not by when the safety department opened the case.

Email bodies, attachments, call notes, partner transmissions, literature documents and translations must remain connected to the case. If an inspector cannot reconstruct what was received and when, the case is not audit-ready.

2. Case validity and identifiers

Verify the identifiable patient, identifiable reporter, suspect product and suspected adverse reaction or other reportable safety information. Confirm that identifiers, source country, reporter qualification, primary-source status and duplicate linkage match the available evidence.

Identifiable does not mean fully identified. Equally, a name alone does not automatically establish a reliable reporter if the source appears fictitious or cannot support the existence of a real person.

3. Patient and reporter data

Check age, date of birth, sex, weight, medical history, pregnancy status, reporter type, contactability and country. Distinguish reported, calculated, estimated, masked, unknown and not applicable data. These states must not be silently converted into one another.

4. Product exposure and treatment chronology

Confirm medicinal-product identity, role, formulation, route, dose, frequency, indication, therapy dates, batch information and action taken. Event onset must be evaluated against exposure. A dechallenge cannot be positive if the medicine continued unchanged, and a rechallenge cannot be claimed without documented re-exposure.

5. Event and product coding

Medical-event coding should use the controlled current version of MedDRA. Product coding should follow the governed product dictionary and, where used, WHODrug Global.

The code must represent the documented concept—not a diagnosis imagined from partial symptoms. Under-coding loses clinically relevant information; over-coding creates unsupported certainty. QC must compare verbatim text, selected term, specificity, hierarchy and duplicate-event handling.

6. Seriousness, severity and outcome

Verify that each seriousness criterion is supported. Do not convert the adjective “severe” into regulatory seriousness. Check hospitalisation dates, interventions, disability, congenital outcomes, death information, medical importance and event outcome against the source and narrative.

7. Narrative quality

The narrative should allow a competent reader to reconstruct source, patient context, exposure, event chronology, investigations, treatment, action taken, outcome, reporter assessment and company assessment. It must not invent chronology, imply causality that was not reported or conceal conflicting information.

8. Cross-field consistency

  • Do event onset and therapy dates support the recorded temporal relationship?
  • Does the narrative agree with structured seriousness, outcome and action-taken fields?
  • Are laboratory results, units, normal ranges and dates represented accurately?
  • Are suspect, interacting, concomitant and treatment-product roles consistent?
  • Are reporter and company causality assessments distinguishable?
  • Does the case version contain all relevant follow-up without losing earlier evidence?

9. Regulatory classification and submission readiness

QC should confirm initial versus follow-up status, seriousness, expectedness, reporting category, destinations and due dates. The review must use the correct effective product and market configuration. Technical completeness cannot compensate for a wrong regulatory decision.

The transmitted message must also comply with the applicable ICH E2B(R3) specification and regional rules such as the EMA electronic-reporting framework.

How QC findings should be classified

LevelMeaningExamples
CriticalMay affect validity, patient safety, expedited reporting, destination or complianceMissed serious event; incorrect day zero; wrong suspect; unsupported invalidation
MajorMaterially affects accuracy or interpretationContradictory chronology; omitted test result; incorrect coding specificity
MinorLimited immediate impact but requires correction or coachingNon-material wording, formatting or metadata defect

Finding severity should be based on potential impact, not reviewer preference. Every finding should have a correction, responsible person, completion time and audit trail. Trends should drive training, system controls, SOP updates and corrective or preventive action.

Risk-based QC is not reduced accountability

A risk-based model can vary review depth according to case seriousness, complexity, product maturity, emerging issues, jurisdiction, processor performance and error history. It should never create blind spots around validity, day zero, seriousness, reportability or submission destination.

A low QC sampling rate is defensible only when measured evidence shows that the process reliably controls critical error—not because the organisation wants higher throughput.

What AI can support in ICSR QC

Automation can compare fields, detect missing data, validate dates, identify contradictions, check dictionaries and E2B structure, and prioritise complex cases. AI can extract chronology, compare narratives with source documents and suggest potential review findings.

But an AI model can also create fluent, unsupported medical conclusions. It must operate within a defined and validated context of use, expose the evidence behind each flag, preserve human override and escalate uncertainty. False negatives require particular attention because a missed serious or reportable feature can remain invisible.

Practical case-QC checklist

  • All source items, translations and receipt dates are attributable.
  • Validity criteria and case identifiers are supported.
  • Patient and reporter data match the source.
  • Product roles, exposure, indications and therapy dates are correct.
  • Events and products are coded to governed dictionaries without unsupported inference.
  • Seriousness is supported and distinguished from severity.
  • Narrative, source and structured fields tell the same story.
  • Follow-up changes are captured without losing earlier evidence.
  • Expectedness, reportability, destinations and due dates are correct.
  • E2B validation, transmission status and acknowledgements are controlled.
  • Findings, rework, overrides and approvals are auditable.

Frequently asked questions

What is ICSR quality control?

It is the systematic verification that a processed safety case accurately represents its source and is complete, consistent, traceable and procedurally compliant before submission.

Is QC the same as medical review?

No. QC primarily confirms fidelity and consistency. Medical review evaluates clinical coherence and consequential medical assessments.

Can the processor perform QC?

Models vary, but sufficient independence and compensating controls are needed to reduce confirmation bias and prove objective review.

Does E2B validation prove case quality?

No. It proves technical or business-rule conformance; it does not prove that chronology, seriousness, coding or medical interpretation are correct.

Can AI replace ICSR QC?

AI can support comparison and detection, but consequential findings require governed human accountability and validated performance.

Conclusion

A quality case is not one that merely reaches submission. It is one that carries faithful evidence, consistent data, visible uncertainty and an auditable decision path. That standard protects the individual case—and every signal, aggregate report and benefit–risk conclusion built from it.

If the source says one thing and the database implies another, the database is not complete. It is wrong.
