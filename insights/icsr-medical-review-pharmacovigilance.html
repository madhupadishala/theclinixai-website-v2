ICSR Quality, Medical Review & Submission

Medical Review of ICSRs: Seriousness, Causality, Expectedness and Clinical Coherence

Understand ICSR medical review, including chronology, diagnosis, seriousness, expectedness, causality, follow-up and AI governance.

TheClinixAI PV Science & Operations 2026-07-236 min read

A medically fluent narrative can still describe a clinically incoherent case. The event may precede exposure. A positive dechallenge may be selected while treatment continued. An expected event may be assessed against the wrong reference document. A serious outcome may be hidden behind a mild-looking term.

Medical review is not the final signature on a processed case. It is the point at which the organisation accepts scientific accountability for what the case means.

The medical reviewer must protect two boundaries at the same time: never ignore a clinically important pattern, and never convert incomplete information into a diagnosis or causal conclusion that the source cannot support.

The operational foundation is described in EMA GVP Module VI and harmonised post-approval safety guidance available through the ICH efficacy-guideline framework. Medical judgement must also follow effective company procedures, product information and regional requirements.

What medical review must accomplish

Medical review determines whether the clinical story, coding and assessments are coherent, appropriately cautious and defensible. It should evaluate the whole case, not approve isolated database selections.

  • Reconstruct the chronology of exposure, onset, intervention, withdrawal, rechallenge and outcome.
  • Determine whether reported diagnoses and selected medical concepts are supported.
  • Assess seriousness and medical importance without confusing seriousness with intensity.
  • Evaluate expectedness against the correct effective reference safety information.
  • Evaluate causality while preserving reporter and company assessments separately.
  • Identify alternative explanations, missing evidence and medically meaningful follow-up.
  • Confirm that the narrative communicates the clinical course without inference beyond the source.

Clinical coherence begins with chronology

A temporal relationship is necessary for many causal interpretations, but temporal sequence alone is not proof. The reviewer should verify first dose, last dose, dose changes, onset, worsening, treatment, dechallenge, rechallenge and recovery. Uncertain dates should remain uncertain.

Chronology also reveals errors that fields conceal. If liver-test abnormalities began before exposure, the case may remain valid if a reporter suspected the medicine, but the company’s medical assessment should not imply that exposure initiated the event. If symptoms recurred before re-exposure, the claimed positive rechallenge requires challenge.

Diagnosis: preserve the source, test the evidence

A diagnosis may be explicitly reported, confirmed through investigations, provisionally suspected or inferred from a pattern. These states are not equivalent. The source diagnosis should be preserved, while coding and narrative wording should make the level of certainty clear.

The reviewer should recognise medically important patterns—such as mucosal involvement with blistering, hepatic injury patterns or symptoms compatible with anaphylaxis—without turning possibility into confirmed diagnosis. The correct action may be targeted follow-up, additional coding under controlled convention or escalation.

Seriousness is not severity

Severity describes intensity. Seriousness is a regulatory classification based on outcomes or interventions such as death, life threat, hospitalisation, disability, congenital anomaly or another medically important condition. A severe migraine may remain non-serious; a clinically subtle arrhythmia may be serious.

The EMA Important Medical Events list can support consistent identification of events that may require medical seriousness consideration. It does not eliminate case-specific judgement or prove seriousness automatically.

Expectedness requires the correct reference

Expectedness is not determined by general medical knowledge or by whether the event is common. It depends on the applicable effective reference safety information for the product and reporting context. Term specificity, nature, severity and outcome can affect the decision.

The reviewer must know which version was effective at the relevant time. An event appearing in a current label may have been unlisted when the case became reportable. Conversely, a broad class statement may not cover a clinically distinct presentation.

Causality is structured reasoning—not a score

Evidence domainQuestions for medical review
TemporalityDid exposure precede the event? Is latency compatible?
Dechallenge/rechallengeDid the event change after withdrawal or re-exposure, and is that change documented?
Biological plausibilityIs the pattern compatible with pharmacology, mechanism or known class effects?
Alternative causesCould disease, infection, procedures, concomitant therapy or background risk explain the event?
Objective evidenceDo tests, imaging, pathology or specialist assessments support the concept?
Prior knowledgeIs the association known, emerging, labelled or biologically unexpected?

Reporter causality and company causality must remain distinguishable. A company may disagree with the reporter, but the reporter’s judgement cannot be erased. Absence of an assessment must not be converted into “not related.”

Dechallenge and rechallenge: common traps

  • Improvement after treatment discontinuation is not necessarily positive dechallenge if the natural disease course or treatment of the event explains recovery.
  • A medicine must actually be stopped or meaningfully reduced before a dechallenge conclusion is made.
  • Rechallenge requires documented re-exposure; persistence of symptoms is not rechallenge.
  • A negative rechallenge may be uninterpretable if dose, latency or concomitant treatments changed.
  • For long half-life products, improvement may not occur immediately after withdrawal.

Medical follow-up should change the evidence

A generic follow-up form can collect many fields without resolving the key uncertainty. Medical review should identify questions capable of changing validity, seriousness, expectedness, causality, diagnosis, outcome or regulatory action.

  • Hepatic events: baseline and serial values, upper limits of normal, competing causes, imaging, viral studies, action taken and recovery.
  • Severe cutaneous reactions: morphology, body-surface involvement, mucosal involvement, biopsy, hospitalisation and treatment.
  • Pregnancy: exposure window, gestational age, prenatal testing, outcome and infant follow-up.
  • Death: date and cause, terminal course, relationship to the event and autopsy findings.
  • Medication error or overdose: circumstances, actual exposure, clinical consequences, intervention and preventability.

Narrative review: the clinical story must remain honest

The narrative should integrate chronology, investigation, treatment and outcome while preserving uncertainty. It must not imply a diagnosis, dechallenge or causality that was never established. Conflicting dates should be described or resolved through follow-up—not silently normalised.

A polished narrative that hides uncertainty is not high quality. It is high-confidence misrepresentation.

Medical review and AI

AI can extract chronology, surface potential contradictions, suggest coding concepts and identify follow-up gaps. It can also produce a convincing diagnosis from incomplete symptoms, infer causality from sequence and confuse severity with seriousness.

A safe model must show its source evidence, state uncertainty, operate within approved knowledge and escalate consequential decisions. Medical accountability remains with qualified humans. The system should support reasoning, not manufacture it.

Medical-review checklist

  • Exposure and event chronology is clinically possible and internally consistent.
  • Diagnoses and codes reflect the level of evidence available.
  • Seriousness criteria and medical importance are supported.
  • Expectedness uses the correct effective reference safety information.
  • Reporter and company causality are separated.
  • Dechallenge and rechallenge conclusions are evidence-based.
  • Alternative explanations and concomitant therapies are considered.
  • Follow-up questions are medically targeted.
  • Narrative and structured assessments communicate the same clinical story.
  • Uncertainty, limitations and overrides are visible in the audit trail.

Frequently asked questions

What is medical review in pharmacovigilance?

It is the qualified clinical evaluation of an ICSR’s chronology, diagnosis, seriousness, expectedness, causality, alternative explanations, follow-up needs and narrative coherence.

Is every severe adverse event serious?

No. Severity describes intensity; seriousness is based on defined outcomes or medical importance.

Does a positive temporal relationship prove causality?

No. It supports consideration but must be evaluated with plausibility, dechallenge, rechallenge, alternative causes and objective evidence.

Can a company change reporter causality?

The company can record its own assessment, but the reporter’s assessment should remain preserved and distinguishable.

Can AI conduct medical review?

It can assist with evidence extraction and consistency checks, but consequential medical conclusions require governed human accountability.

Conclusion

Medical review does not exist to make every case sound medically complete. It exists to make every conclusion proportionate to the evidence. The reviewer’s responsibility is not to choose the strongest diagnosis or the safest regulatory answer. It is to choose the most defensible interpretation while making uncertainty visible.

A pharmacovigilance decision is strong only when another competent reviewer can trace the same evidence and understand how the conclusion was reached.
